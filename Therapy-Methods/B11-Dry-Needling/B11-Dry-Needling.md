# B11 — Dry Needling

> **Scope:** The insertion of solid filiform needles into myofascial trigger points
> (or related musculoskeletal targets) to produce pain relief and restore function,
> guided by Western biomedical theory. Includes deep dry needling (DDN), superficial
> dry needling (SDN), intramuscular stimulation (IMS), Fu's subcutaneous needling
> (FSN), and dry needling with electrical stimulation (DNWES).
>
> **Central question:** Dry needling uses the SAME needle as acupuncture at locations
> that overlap 93.3% with classical acupoints (Dorsher 2008). If the tools and targets
> are nearly identical, what — if anything — makes dry needling a distinct modality?
> This file investigates that question through evidence and mechanism analysis.
>
> **Evidence scale:** ~321 RCTs (PubMed mid-2026), ~200+ since 2018, ~80-120
> systematic reviews, NO dedicated Cochrane review. 468 publications 2004-2024
> (Deng et al. 2024 bibliometric analysis). 18+ conditions studied, predominantly
> low-to-moderate quality evidence. TIER 1 lite (2 sessions).
>
> **Unique dimension:** Dry needling is the ONLY therapy in Track B with a
> significant sociopolitical layer — an active scope-of-practice debate between
> physical therapists and acupuncturists over who may insert needles and under
> what theoretical framework. This debate shapes the research landscape itself.
>
> **Relationship to B01 (Acupuncture):** SAME needle (FDA Class II solid filiform),
> SAME anatomical targets (93.3% overlap), DIFFERENT theoretical framework.
> B01 §7.2 contains the initial comparison; B11 provides the DN-side perspective
> and expands the analysis.
>
> **Relationship to A08 (Muscle-Tendon-Joint):** A08 §3.5-§3.6 contains the
> foundational trigger point mechanism (Simons' hypothesis, Shah milieu, Hocking
> 2023, Strickland 2026). A08 §4.2 contains the DN therapy row. B11 §5 builds on
> this foundation — does NOT repeat it.
>
> **Framing:** Dry needling has NO traditional theoretical framework (unlike B01-B03).
> It is entirely Western biomedical from inception. However, the historical relationship
> to acupuncture — and the question of whether DN IS acupuncture under a different
> name — is addressed directly and honestly.
>
> **Track A cross-reference:** 6 of 9 Track A systems are directly engaged.
> A08 (Musculoskeletal) is the PRIMARY system; A01 (PNS) provides the neural
> mechanism framework; A02 (ANS) for segmental autonomic effects.

## §1 — DEFINITION & ORIGIN

### §1.1 — What Dry Needling Is

```
🟢 PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION:

  Dry needling is a therapeutic intervention involving the insertion of thin,
  solid filiform needles (the same FDA-classified "acupuncture needles") into
  myofascial trigger points, taut muscle bands, or related musculoskeletal
  targets to produce pain relief and restore function. No substance is injected
  (hence "dry" — distinguishing it from "wet" injection therapy).

  THE CORE EMPIRICAL OBSERVATION:
    Inserting a needle into a myofascial trigger point elicits a LOCAL TWITCH
    RESPONSE (LTR) — a brief involuntary contraction followed by relaxation.
    After the LTR, the trigger point's spontaneous electrical activity (SEA)
    decreases, the local biochemical milieu normalizes (Shah 2005, 2008),
    and the patient reports pain reduction.

  KEY DISTINCTION FROM WET NEEDLING (INJECTION):
    Lewit 1979: "the common denominator was puncture by the needle and NOT
    the anesthetic employed." The needle itself is the therapeutic agent.
    Cummings & White 2001: no advantage of injection over dry needle.

## §2 — STIMULATION TYPE

### §2.1 — Deep Dry Needling (DDN) — The Dominant Technique

```
🟢 DEEP DRY NEEDLING:

  The most widely practiced form. Needle penetrates THROUGH skin and
  subcutaneous tissue INTO the muscle, specifically targeting the
  myofascial trigger point within a taut band.

  TECHNIQUE — HONG'S FAST-IN-FAST-OUT (PISTONING):
    ① Identify taut band via flat or pincer palpation
    ② Locate trigger point (maximally tender/nodular spot)
    ③ Clean skin; insert needle through guide tube
    ④ Advance needle into trigger point
    ⑤ Perform rapid in-and-out movements (~1 Hz cadence, ~30 seconds)
    ⑥ Target: elicit multiple LOCAL TWITCH RESPONSES (LTRs)
    ⑦ Continue pistoning until LTRs cease (= ACh depleted at endplate)
    ⑧ Remove needle; apply pressure hemostasis

  LOCAL TWITCH RESPONSE (LTR):
    → Involuntary SPINAL CORD REFLEX
    → Visible brief contraction of the taut band
    → Mechanism: needle contacts trigger point nidus →
      rapid depolarization of involved muscle fibers →
      spinal reflex arc (monosynaptic or oligosynaptic)
    → After LTR: endplate noise (SEA) decreases
    → Shah milieu: CGRP ↓, SP ↓, β-endorphin ↑
    → Multiple LTRs correlate with better outcomes (Hong 1994)
    → ⚠ BUT: LTR may NOT be strictly necessary (§9.5, §10 Q1)

  "SPARROW PECKING":
    → Functionally identical to pistoning — solid filiform needles
      manipulated in and out of trigger points
    → Term derives from visual resemblance to a sparrow pecking
    → Same mechanism: repeated insertion/withdrawal to elicit LTRs

  PARAMETERS:
    Needle depth:     10-50mm (depends on target muscle + patient BMI)
    Treatment time:   ~30 seconds to several minutes per trigger point
    Points/session:   Typically 1-6 trigger points
    Session frequency: 1-3× per week
    Total sessions:   3-6 typical course (varies by condition)
    Post-needling:    Soreness expected 24-72 hours (48-52% of patients)

  🟢 Hong 1994 (Am J Phys Med Rehabil): published, peer-reviewed
  🟢 Shah 2005, 2008 (biochemical milieu): published
  🟢 DDN technique: established clinical protocol
```

### §2.2 — Superficial Dry Needling (SDN) — Baldry's Approach

```
🟢🟡 SUPERFICIAL DRY NEEDLING:

  Developed by Peter Baldry (UK physician). Needle inserted ONLY 5-10mm
  into subcutaneous tissue overlying the trigger point — does NOT
  penetrate into the muscle itself.

  TECHNIQUE:
    ① Identify trigger point by palpation
    ② Insert needle (0.3mm diameter, 30mm length) only 5-10mm deep
    ③ Leave in place ~30 seconds
    ④ If tenderness persists → reinsert for 2-3 minutes
    ⑤ Remove

  PROPOSED MECHANISM:
    → Aδ fiber stimulation in subcutaneous tissue
    → A-delta input blocks noxious C-fiber transmission from
      trigger point (gate control mechanism, A01 §3.1)
    → Does NOT require LTR (needle does not reach taut band)

  BALDRY'S CLINICAL FINDING:
    → 90% of patients with uncomplicated nociceptive pain
      responded to superficial needling alone
    → Reserved DDN for complicated cases with nerve root pain
    → = Challenged the assumption that deep penetration is necessary

  DEEP vs SUPERFICIAL — EVIDENCE:
    Cummings & White 2001: reviewed 23 trials, concluded both
      deep and superficial effective; no clear superiority of deep
    Baldry 2002 (Acupuncture in Medicine): argued SDN safer,
      less painful, equally effective for most patients
    2025 SR (neck pain): no clinically meaningful difference between
      deep and superficial for cervical trigger points
    ⚠ This finding CHALLENGES the centrality of LTR — if superficial
      (which cannot reach the endplate) works equally well, the
      LTR/ACh depletion mechanism may not be the primary pathway

  ADVANTAGES:
    → Lower post-needling soreness
    → Lower pneumothorax risk (needle stays in subcutaneous layer)
    → Better tolerated by needle-sensitive patients

  🟢 Baldry 2002 (Acupuncture in Medicine): published
  🟢 Cummings & White 2001 (Archives PM&R): systematic review
  🟡 "No clinically meaningful difference": 2025 SR, moderate quality
```

### §2.3 — Intramuscular Stimulation (IMS) — Gunn's Approach

```
🟢🟡 INTRAMUSCULAR STIMULATION (IMS):

  Developed by C. Chan Gunn (University of British Columbia) in the
  1970s. A parallel tradition of dry needling based on a DIFFERENT
  theoretical model from Travell-Simons.

  GUNN'S MODEL — NEUROPATHY/RADICULOPATHY:
    → All chronic myofascial pain results from peripheral NEUROPATHY
      or radiculopathy (nerve root irritation)
    → Based on Cannon & Rosenblueth's Law of Denervation
      Supersensitivity (1949): when a nerve is impaired, target
      tissues become hypersensitive to the remaining neural input
    → Shortened muscles = sign of denervation supersensitivity
    → = Different theoretical starting point from trigger point model

  TECHNIQUE:
    → Slow needle insertion into shortened muscle bands
    → Characteristic "NEEDLE GRASP" = spastic muscle grips needle
    → Targets BOTH:
      ① Local painful muscle (the presenting complaint)
      ② Paraspinal muscles at the corresponding SPINAL SEGMENT
         (addressing the root cause per Gunn's model)
    → Needle retained until muscle relaxes or is gently withdrawn

  KEY DISTINCTION FROM STANDARD DDN:
    Standard DDN → targets specific trigger point within ONE muscle
    IMS → targets the SEGMENTAL NERVE DYSFUNCTION underlying
          multiple muscle findings; always includes paraspinals

  EVIDENCE:
    → Primarily clinical case series and observational data
    → Fewer RCTs compared to standard DDN
    → iSTOP (Institute for the Study and Treatment of Pain, UBC)
      continues to teach and research IMS
    → = Mechanistically distinct model with its own clinical tradition

  🟢 Gunn 1996/1997 (published manual and framework)
  🟡 IMS model: plausible but less empirically tested than trigger point model
```

### §2.4 — Fu's Subcutaneous Needling (FSN)

```
🟡 FU'S SUBCUTANEOUS NEEDLING (FSN):

  Invented 1996 by Professor Fu Zhonghua. A DISTINCT technique
  operating in a different tissue layer from both DDN and SDN.

  UNIQUE FEATURES:
    → Uses a modified TROCAR (dual-structure needle), not standard
      filiform — thicker with a protective casing
    → Inserted into SUBCUTANEOUS connective tissue layer between
      skin and muscle — NOT into muscle
    → Needle positioned AWAY from the lesion site
    → Manipulated with a characteristic "SWAYING" movement
    → Based on fascial continuity theory (→ A03)

  TISSUE LAYER COMPARISON:
    DDN:  skin → subcutaneous → INTO MUSCLE (trigger point nidus)
    SDN:  skin → 5-10mm into SUBCUTANEOUS (not reaching muscle)
    FSN:  skin → subcutaneous CONNECTIVE TISSUE (distinct approach)

  EVIDENCE:
    → RCT for knee OA (Chiu et al. 2022, Am J Phys Med Rehabil)
    → RCT for lateral epicondylalgia (2022)
    → Fewer needles needed; less painful than DDN
    → = Emerging modality with promising but limited evidence

  🟡 FSN: fewer RCTs than DDN; mechanism hypothesis-level
  🟢 Chiu et al. 2022 RCT: published, peer-reviewed
```

### §2.5 — Dry Needling with Electrical Stimulation (DNWES)

```
🟢🟡 ELECTRICAL DRY NEEDLING:

  Combines filiform needle insertion with passage of electrical
  current through the needles. SIGNIFICANT crossover with
  electroacupuncture (EA) technique from B01.

  VARIANTS:
    ① Percutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation (PENS):
       → Low-intensity current through inserted needles
       → Frequencies: 1-4 Hz (endorphin release) or
         80-100 Hz (dynorphin/enkephalin) — SAME frequency-
         dependent opioid release as EA (→ B01 §5, A06 §1.1)
    ② Percutaneous Electrolysis:
       → Continuous GALVANIC current (~5 seconds)
       → Produces electrochemical reaction in targeted tissue
       → Used primarily for tendinopathy
    ③ Periosteal Electrical Dry Needling:
       → Electrical stimulation through needles contacting periosteum
       → Fernández-de-las-Peñas et al. 2018 multicenter RCT for knee OA

  EVIDENCE:
    → 2023 SR: 5 RCTs on DNWES for shoulder musculoskeletal pain
    → Growing evidence base, particularly for spasticity
    → May extend duration of therapeutic effects
    → ⚠ The overlap with electroacupuncture is SUBSTANTIAL —
      same needle, same current, same frequency-dependent opioid
      release. The distinction is theoretical framework, not physics.

  🟢 Fernández-de-las-Peñas et al. 2018 (Clinical J Pain): RCT
  🟡 DN+ES mechanism: extrapolated from EA research (B01)
```

### §2.6 — Periosteal Pecking

```
🟡 PERIOSTEAL PECKING:

  Originally described by Felix Mann. Needle repeatedly thrusted
  onto bone periosteum (NOT into muscle tissue).

  MECHANISM:
    → Periosteum is richly innervated with nociceptors
    → Needle contact stimulates periosteal mechanoreceptors
    → Local blood flow increase
    → Endogenous pain-relieving substance release
    → Periosteal reaction may stimulate bone healing pathways

  APPLICATIONS:
    → Tendinopathies (tennis/golfer's elbow)
    → Osteoarthritis (hip, knee)
    → Periosteal pain at enthesis sites

## §3 — APPLICATION POINTS & AREAS

### §3.1 — Target: Myofascial Trigger Points

```
🟢 PALPATION-BASED POINT SELECTION:

  Unlike acupuncture (meridian-based), dry needling selects targets
  through MANUAL PALPATION:

  IDENTIFICATION PROCESS:
    ① Clinician identifies TAUT BAND in skeletal muscle
       → Abnormally tight strip of muscle fibers
       → Palpable via flat grip (skin against underlying tissue)
         or pincer grip (lifting muscle between fingers)
    ② Within taut band, locate TRIGGER POINT (trigger point)
       → Hyperirritable spot: maximally tender/nodular
       → Reproduces patient's pain complaint (active trigger point)
       → Or painful only on palpation (latent trigger point)
    ③ Confirm by pain referral pattern
       → Pressing on trigger point reproduces the patient's REFERRED PAIN
       → Patterns documented by Travell & Simons (→ A08 §3.6)
    ④ Optionally confirm by LTR ("jump sign")
       → Snapping palpation across taut band → visible twitch

  TWO TYPES OF TRIGGER POINTS:
    ACTIVE: spontaneously painful, produces referred pain,
            motor dysfunction, autonomic phenomena
    LATENT: painful only on palpation, restricts ROM,
            can become active under stress/overuse
    → Full mechanism: A08 §3.5 (Simons, Shah, Hocking, Strickland)

  IMS POINT SELECTION (different from above):
    → Gunn model adds PARASPINAL muscles at the spinal segment
      corresponding to the patient's complaint
    → Addresses "root cause" (nerve root) not just local symptom
    → Always treats BOTH local AND paraspinal targets

  FSN POINT SELECTION:
    → Target area identified but needle placed AWAY from lesion
    → Inserted into subcutaneous tissue of a different region
    → Based on fascial connectivity theory (→ A03)

  🟢 trigger point identification: established clinical assessment
  🟢 Travell & Simons 1983/1992 (referred pain patterns)
  🟡 IMS segmental model: plausible, fewer controlled studies
```

### §3.3 — The 93.3% Overlap with Acupoints

```
🟢🟡 TRIGGER POINTS AND ACUPOINTS — THE OVERLAP QUESTION:

  ⭐ THE DATA:

  MELZACK 1977 (Pain, 3(1):3-23) — THE ORIGINAL STUDY:
    → Compared 56 trigger points with TCM acupuncture points
    → Found 100% ANATOMIC correspondence (every trigger point had an
      acupoint within 3 cm)
    → Found 71% CLINICAL correspondence (referred pain patterns
      matched acupoint indications)
    → Concluded these likely "represent the same phenomenon"

  BIRCH 2003 (J Alt Complement Med) — CRITICAL REANALYSIS:
    → Argued the 71% figure was inflated
    → 61% of acupoints Melzack examined were NOT recommended for pain
    → Revised estimate: only 18-19% truly correlate (or ~40% at most)
    → Proposed that ASHI POINTS (tender/palpation-based) — not
      classical meridian points — are the real analog to trigger points

  DORSHER 2006-2009 — THE DEFINITIVE EXPANSION:
    → Compared 255 "most common" trigger points (from Travell-Simons
      Manual) against 361 classical acupuncture points
    → 238/255 (93.3%) had anatomically corresponding acupoint
    → 94% pain indication match (221/238 pairs)
    → 93% somatovisceral correspondence (56/60 non-pain trigger points)
    → 91% correlation between trigger point referred-pain patterns and
      meridian distributions (Dorsher 2009, J Pain)
    → Probability of chance overlap: ~1 in 7 TRILLION

  DORSHER vs BIRCH — RESOLUTION:
    → Dorsher 2008 directly rebutted Birch's critique
    → Key difference: Dorsher compared ALL common trigger points (255) vs
      Birch's reanalysis of Melzack's smaller subset (56)
    → Both agree that ASHI POINTS (阿是穴) are the closest analog
    → Sun Simiao, 652 CE: "Ah yes!" = patient's exclamation when
      tender point found. = Palpation-based, not meridian-based.
    → = The SELECTION METHOD is what overlaps, not the theory.

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  IMPLICATION FOR B11:                                                    │
  │                                                                        │
  │  If 93.3% of trigger point locations ARE acupuncture points,           │
  │  then practitioners of DN and acupuncture are — in most cases —        │
  │  inserting the same needle at the same anatomical location.             │
  │                                                                        │
  │  The difference is:                                                     │
  │    → HOW the point was selected (palpation vs pattern diagnosis)       │
  │    → WHY the point was selected (NMJ dysfunction vs Qi flow)           │
  │    → WHAT is done with the needle (pistoning vs gentle manipulation)   │
  │    → HOW MUCH of the system is addressed (local vs systemic)           │
  │                                                                        │
  │  This does NOT mean DN = acupuncture. But it means the anatomical      │
  │  SUBSTRATE is largely shared. The distinction is THEORY and APPROACH,  │
  │  not ANATOMY.                                                           │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  🟢 Melzack 1977, Dorsher 2006/2008/2009: published, peer-reviewed
  🟢 Birch 2003: published reanalysis
  🟡 "Distinction is theory, not anatomy": synthesis from data
```

## §4 — DOCUMENTED CLINICAL EFFECTS

### §4.1 — Evidence Tiering Methodology

```
  Same tiering methodology as B01-B03:

  TIER 1: Multiple positive systematic reviews/meta-analyses with
          MODERATE quality evidence (GRADE)
  TIER 2: At least one positive SR/MA with LOW-TO-MODERATE evidence,
          OR multiple RCTs with consistent direction
  TIER 3: Limited evidence — few RCTs, case series, mixed results,
          OR large reviews finding insufficient evidence despite
          substantial study count
  TIER 4: Emerging — case reports, registered trials, pilot studies

  IMPORTANT CAVEAT FOR ALL DRY NEEDLING EVIDENCE:
    No condition reaches HIGH-certainty evidence by GRADE criteria.
    The entire DN evidence base sits at very-low to moderate quality.
    This is consistent across B01-B03 but is particularly notable
    for DN given the active scope-of-practice debate (→ §9.3).
```

### §4.2 — TIER 1: Moderate Evidence

```
🟢 CONDITIONS WITH MULTIPLE POSITIVE SYSTEMATIC REVIEWS:

  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    The CORE indication — dry needling's primary target.

    Llamas-Ramos et al. 2014/2015 (Archives PM&R):
      20 RCTs, 839 patients. DN recommended for MPS in neck/shoulders
      for short- and medium-term pain relief.

  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ② NECK PAIN (MECHANICAL/CHRONIC)
  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    Hernández-Secorún et al. 2023 (Pain Research Management):
      14 RCTs (13 in MA). DN improved pain intensity and functional

  ③ LOW BACK PAIN (CHRONIC)

    Liu et al. 2018 (Archives PM&R, 99(1)):
      11 RCTs, 802 patients.
      Pain intensity: SMD = −2.74 (very large, HIGH heterogeneity)
      Pain disability: SMD = −0.76 (moderate)
      DN vs other treatments: SMD = −1.06 (P = .003)

  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

### §4.3 — TIER 2: Low-to-Moderate Evidence

```
🟢🟡 CONDITIONS WITH POSITIVE BUT LIMITED EVIDENCE:

  ① TENSION-TYPE HEADACHE (TTH):
    Pourahmadi et al. 2021 (Physical Therapy, 101(5)):
      11 RCTs combined (TTH, CGH, migraine).
      Pain intensity: very low evidence, NOT better than other Tx.
      Disability: DN significantly BETTER in short term.
      Also improved: headache frequency, QoL, trigger point tenderness, ROM.
    2024 SR (Complement Ther Med): 13 RCTs, 10 in MA.
      Significant decline in disability scores and headache intensity.
      Headache frequency reduced at 1 and 3 months.
    France et al. 2014 (Cephalalgia):
      DN + conventional physiotherapy more useful than DN alone.

  ② CERVICOGENIC HEADACHE (CGH):
    Pourahmadi 2021: DN improved headache frequency, QoL, ROM in CGH.

  ③ SHOULDER PAIN (SUBACROMIAL/IMPINGEMENT):
    Para-García et al. 2022 (Int J Environ Res Public Health):
      5 RCTs, 315 participants. SMD = −0.27 (SMALL, short-term).
    Blanco-Díaz et al. 2022 (Biology): improvements in pain, ROM,
      PPT, DASH/SPADI scores. Low quality evidence.
    Pain Medicine 2022: moderate effect size short-term; NS mid/long.

  ④ KNEE OSTEOARTHRITIS:
    Ughreja & Prem 2021 (J Bodywork Movement Ther):
      9 studies. Periosteal stimulation: significant (P < .00001).

      trigger point DN: contradictory results.
    Jiménez-del-Barrio et al. 2022 (Life):
      7 studies, 291 patients. Pain SMD = −0.76; function SMD = −0.98.
      NO significant differences at medium- or long-term.
      GRADE quality: VERY LOW.
    Tayyab et al. 2026 (Ther Adv Musculoskelet Dis): most recent SR.

  ⑤ TENDINOPATHIES (various):
    2025 SR (Cureus): 4 trials, 255 patients (lateral epicondyle,

    2022 SR (Complement Ther Med): DN as effective as other Tx for
      pain/symptoms up to 6 months.

  ⑥ SPORTS INJURIES:
    2025 SR (Sports Medicine): 24 studies, 580 athletes.
      DN reduced pain severity and increased movement volume.
      Effective and safe for pain/stiffness reduction.

  ⑦ POST-STROKE SPASTICITY:
    Sánchez-Infante et al. 2021 (Pain Medicine):

### §4.4 — TIER 3: Limited/Emerging Evidence

```
🟡🔴 CONDITIONS WITH INSUFFICIENT OR MIXED EVIDENCE:

  ① MIGRAINE:
    Very few DN-specific RCTs. Migraine included as small subgroup
    in broader headache reviews (Pourahmadi 2021). Insufficient
    data to draw migraine-specific conclusions.

  ② TMJ DISORDERS / OROFACIAL PAIN:

  ③ FIBROMYALGIA:

  ④ CARPAL TUNNEL SYNDROME:
    Gascón-García et al. 2025 (J Bodywork Movement Ther):

      ⚠ Short follow-up (12 weeks), no blinding.

    Heliyon 2024: RCT, 36 women, 3 groups (DN/sham/control).

    Mayoral et al. 2013 (Evid Based Complement Alt Med):

  ⑦ HIP PAIN / HIP OA:
    Jiménez-del-Barrio et al. 2022: included with knee OA.

    Short-term positive; NS at medium/long-term.

  ⑧ WHIPLASH-ASSOCIATED DISORDER:
    SR of 6 RCTs: most had serious methodological flaws.
    One RCT: DN + PT had no effect vs sham-DN + PT.
    = Insufficient evidence.

  🟡 TMD "disappointingly little": direct quote from 2024 SR
  🟡🔴 Most Tier 3 conditions: insufficient for clinical recommendation

### §4.6 — The Quality-vs-Quantity Tension

```
🟡 WHEN MANY STUDIES ≠ STRONG EVIDENCE:

    → But small sample sizes (typically n=20-80)

### §9.2 — The Sham/Blinding Challenge

```

### §5.2.1 — L1: Local Tissue Mechanisms (PRIMARY)

```
🟢🟡 L1 — WHERE DRY NEEDLING LIVES:

  This is DN's HOME LEVEL — the trigger point and its immediate
  environment. DN theory places its entire rationale here.
  Timescale: seconds to minutes.

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ① LOCAL TWITCH RESPONSE (LTR) → MECHANICAL DISRUPTION:
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    MECHANISM:
      → Needle contacts trigger point nidus within taut band
      → Rapid depolarization of dysfunctional muscle fibers
      → Spinal cord reflex arc (monosynaptic or oligosynaptic)
      → Brief involuntary contraction of the ENTIRE taut band

      → Patients who achieved LTR → better pain relief

      → ⚠ BUT this is CHALLENGED (→ §9.5, §10 Q1)

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → = REDUCES transmission of ongoing C-fiber pain signals

    → Duration: during needling + minutes to hours after

        endplate → CANNOT produce LTR
      → BUT subcutaneous tissue is richly innervated with Aδ fibers
      → SDN's PRIMARY mechanism is gate control at L2
      → = Explains WHY SDN works despite NOT producing LTR
      → = The LTR (L1) is ONE path; gate control (L2) is ANOTHER

    COMPARISON WITH B01:
      → Acupuncture: De Qi involves Aβ (mechanical) + Aδ + C fiber
      → DN pistoning: primarily Aδ (sharp needle sensation)
      → Both activate gate control, but through different fiber
        profiles and different subjective experiences

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → Reduced excitability of motor neurons in the same myotome
    → Reduced gamma motor neuron drive → decreased muscle spindle
      sensitivity → reduced resting muscle tone
    → = Explains why DN at one trigger point can reduce tenderness
      in ADJACENT trigger points within the same segment

      → Gunn's protocol ALWAYS includes paraspinal needling at

      provides ongoing sensorimotor input maintaining the
      neuroplastic changes initiated by DN

    🟡 Neuroplasticity changes: inferred from spinal cord
    physiology and clinical observation (multiple sessions
    needed). No direct imaging studies of DN-specific dorsal
    horn changes exist.

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ④ SOMATOAUTONOMIC REFLEX (IMS-RELEVANT):
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → Needle at specific spinal segment → somatic afferent input →
      spinal interneurons → preganglionic autonomic neurons →
      organ/vessel response in same segment (Sato 1997, A02 §3.3)
    → Paraspinal DN (IMS) would activate this reflex MAXIMALLY
      (closest to spinal nerve root)
    → Local vasomotor changes: skin temperature changes observed
      after DN in clinical practice (not systematically studied)

    COMPARISON WITH B01:
      → Acupuncture explicitly TARGETS somatovisceral effects
        (e.g., BL20 at T11 → spleen function)
      → DN treats this as an incidental finding, not a goal
      → = Same mechanism, different INTENTION

  🟢 Melzack & Wall 1965 (gate control): foundational
  🟢 Sato 1997 (somatoautonomic reflex): established
  🟡 SDN gate control explanation: mechanistic inference
  🟡 Dorsal horn neuroplasticity: extrapolated from pain science

### §5.2.3 — L3: Supraspinal Mechanisms

```
🟡 L3 — THE MECHANISMS DN ACTIVATES BUT DOESN'T CLAIM:

  Timescale: seconds to minutes.
  Maps to B01 L4 (Brainstem) + partially L5 (Cortical).

  ⭐ CRITICAL DISTINCTION:
    DN theory does NOT typically invoke supraspinal mechanisms.
    The standard DN rationale stops at L1 (trigger point) + L2 (segment).
    But the needle stimulus IS sufficient to activate L3 pathways —
    DN DOES produce supraspinal effects WHETHER OR NOT the
    practitioner's model accounts for them.

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ① DESCENDING INHIBITION (PAG/RVM):
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → Aδ/C fiber input from needle → ascends to PAG
      (Periaqueductal Gray)
    → PAG → RVM (Rostral Ventromedial Medulla):
      ① OFF-cells activated → widespread analgesia
      ② ON-cells inhibited → reduced pain facilitation
    → = BILATERAL analgesia (both sides of body)
    → = Outlasts the stimulation by minutes to hours

    DN EVIDENCE:
      → DN can reduce pain sensitivity at sites REMOTE from
        the needled trigger point (widespread hypoalgesia)
      → This CANNOT be explained by L1 (local) or L2 (segmental)
        mechanisms alone
      → Must involve supraspinal descending inhibition
      → = DN IS activating PAG/RVM whether or not the DN
        practitioner attributes the effect to trigger point theory

    COMPARISON WITH B01:
      → Acupuncture: PAG activation explicitly targeted, confirmed
        by fMRI (Hui et al. 2005: De Qi → limbic deactivation)
      → DN: PAG activation occurs but is theoretically INVISIBLE —
        not part of the clinical rationale

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ② DNIC / CONDITIONED PAIN MODULATION (CPM):
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    "Pain inhibits pain" — a well-characterized phenomenon:
    → Painful stimulus at one site → REDUCES pain at a distant site
    → Mediated by subnucleus reticularis dorsalis → diffuse
      noxious inhibitory controls
    → DN pistoning IS painful (brief, intense) →
      activates DNIC → widespread pain inhibition

    THIS RAISES A FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION:
      → How much of DN's analgesic effect is SPECIFIC (L1: trigger point
        disruption, ACh depletion, milieu normalization)?
      → How much is NON-SPECIFIC (L3: DNIC from any painful
        needle stimulus at any location)?
      → If DNIC is a major component → needling at NON-trigger point sites
        should produce similar analgesia → and it sometimes DOES
        (sham DN studies show partial effects)

    🟡 The DNIC contribution to DN analgesia has not been
    quantified. This is a critical gap (→ §10 Q4).

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ③ ENDOGENOUS OPIOID RELEASE (CENTRAL):
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → Needle pain → PAG → endorphin release centrally
    → β-endorphin, met-enkephalin at brainstem/spinal levels
    → Naloxone partially reverses DN analgesia in some studies
    → = Central opioid component EXISTS

    WHAT DN DOES NOT ENGAGE:
      → EA frequency-dependent opioid prescribing (Han):
        2 Hz → endorphin/enkephalin
        100 Hz → dynorphin
        2/100 Hz alternating → ALL opioids simultaneously
      → This precision is ABSENT from standard DDN (no electrical)
      → DNWES (§2.5) recovers this capability — making DNWES
        mechanistically closest to electroacupuncture

    COMPARISON WITH B01:
      → Acupuncture: central opioid release is a PRIMARY mechanism
        (B01 §5.2.1 L4), deliberately manipulated via EA parameters
      → DN: central opioids released INCIDENTALLY, not targeted

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ④ CENTRAL SENSITIZATION REVERSAL:
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → Chronic trigger points → sustained nociceptive input → central
      sensitization at spinal AND supraspinal levels:
      • Widespread hyperalgesia (lowered pain thresholds everywhere)
      • Allodynia (non-painful stimuli perceived as painful)
      • Expanded cortical pain representation
    → DN series (multiple sessions) → normalized nociceptive
      input from trigger point site → gradual REVERSAL:
      • Decreased widespread hyperalgesia
      • Narrowed pain representation
      • Restored cortical processing patterns

    → Explains the COMBINATION THERAPY superiority pattern:
      DN addresses the PERIPHERAL SOURCE (L1) + begins
      desensitization (L2-L3); exercise provides ongoing
      SENSORIMOTOR INPUT that maintains the reversal

    🟡 Central sensitization reversal: inferred from pain
    neuroscience principles, supported by clinical pattern
    (widespread hypoalgesia after DN), but no DN-specific
    neuroimaging studies confirm cortical changes.

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  WHAT DN DOES NOT ENGAGE AT L3:                             │
  │                                                              │
  │  → PROKR2 pathway (Liu 2021): PROKR2+ neurons in deep      │
  │    fascia → NTS → DMV → vagal-adrenal anti-inflammatory     │
  │    50% decrease TNF-α, 50% decrease IL-6                    │
  │    = Requires specific EA parameters (low-intensity limb)    │
  │    = ABSENT from standard DN (no EA, no specific targeting)  │
  │                                                              │
  │  → Limbic deactivation pattern (Hui 2005): specific to      │
  │    De Qi needle sensation, NOT to pistoning/LTR sensation    │
  │    DN → sharp pain → may produce OPPOSITE pattern            │
  │    (amygdala ACTIVATION, not deactivation)                   │
  │                                                              │
  │  → DMN modulation: no DN-specific fMRI evidence             │
  │                                                              │
  │  = L3 is PARTIALLY activated by DN but SUBSTANTIALLY less   │
  │    than by acupuncture, which deliberately targets these     │
  │    supraspinal pathways through parameter selection           │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  🟢 PAG/RVM descending inhibition: established neuroscience
  🟢 DNIC/CPM: well-characterized phenomenon
  🟢 Han opioid frequency-dependence: published (B01 §5)
  🟡 DN-specific supraspinal activation: limited direct evidence
  🔴 DNIC contribution quantification: unknown
```

### §5.2.4 — L4: Connective Tissue / Fascial Mechanisms

```
🟡 L4 — THE FASCIAL DIMENSION (VARIANT-DEPENDENT):

  Timescale: seconds (mechanical) to days (remodeling).
  Cross-cuts B01 L1 — fascia is local tissue but with CONTINUITY
  properties that extend its effects beyond the insertion site.

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ① COLLAGEN WINDING (Langevin 2001):
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → Needle rotation in tissue → collagen fibers WIND around
      needle shaft → "needle grasp" phenomenon
    → Mechanical signal propagated through collagen network
    → Fibroblasts detect tension → cytoskeletal remodeling:
      Rho GTPase activation → actin stress fiber formation
      Rac activation → lamellipodia extension
    → Mechanosensitive ion channels: Piezo1, TRPV4 activated
    → Duration: sustained mechanical signal during retention

    APPLICATION TO DN VARIANTS:
      → DDN pistoning: MINIMAL collagen winding — needle moves
        too rapidly for sustained fiber engagement
      → IMS needle grasp: MAXIMAL collagen winding — slow
        insertion, muscle grips needle, sustained retention
      → FSN swaying: LATERAL collagen engagement in
        subcutaneous layer (different orientation)
      → = Different DN variants engage fascia DIFFERENTLY

    SHARED WITH ACUPUNCTURE (B01 L1):
      → Acupuncture needle retention (20-30 min) with gentle
        rotation = OPTIMAL for collagen winding
      → DN pistoning = SUB-OPTIMAL for this mechanism
      → IMS is CLOSER to acupuncture in fascial engagement
      → = Another dimension where technique overlap matters

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ② FIBROBLAST REMODELING:
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    → Mechanical stimulation → fibroblast ATP release →
      purinergic signaling cascade (links back to L1 ④)
    → Crane et al. 2012 pathway (→ B03 §5.2.4):
      Mechanical stimulus → FAK activation → ERK1/2 →
      NF-κB INHIBITION → reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines
      + PGC-1α ↑ → mitochondrial biogenesis
    → = Anti-inflammatory + regenerative signaling from
      mechanical stimulation alone (no specific drug needed)
    → ⚠ Crane's data was from massage (cyclic compressive
      loading), not needling — extrapolation to DN is plausible
      but unconfirmed for needle-specific mechanics

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ③ FSN'S SUBCUTANEOUS MECHANISM:
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    FSN (Fu's Subcutaneous Needling) operates EXCLUSIVELY at L4:
    → Modified trocar in subcutaneous connective tissue layer
    → Does NOT enter muscle → CANNOT produce LTR
    → Does NOT target specific trigger point
    → "Swaying" movement → sustained mechanical input to fascia
    → Based on fascial continuity theory (→ A03):
      subcutaneous fascia forms continuous network →
      mechanical signal at one location affects distant tissue

    FSN MECHANISM HYPOTHESIS:
      → Swaying → mechanical wave in subcutaneous layer →
      → Fascial mechanoreceptors stimulated →
      → Proprioceptive input altered → muscle tone adjusted →
      → Hyaluronic acid (HA) metabolism stimulated →
        HA viscosity reduced → tissue gliding restored
      → = Thixotropic effect similar to B03's light pressure
        mechanism (B03 §5.2.2)

    ⚠ FSN mechanism is the LEAST understood of all DN variants.
    Clinical results (Chiu 2022 knee OA, lateral epicondylalgia
    RCT) exist but mechanistic explanation remains at 🔴 level.

    → = FSN may be closer to MASSAGE (fascial release) than
      to DDN (trigger point deactivation) in mechanism

  🟢 Langevin 2001 (collagen winding): published
  🟢 Crane et al. 2012 (mechanotransduction): published
  🟡 DN variant-specific fascial engagement: analytical comparison
  🔴 FSN mechanism: hypothesis-level understanding
```

    → DN: session goal = deactivate specific trigger point

    ⑤ Head-to-head DN vs acupuncture should show SIMILAR short-term

    → BUT: the biological mechanisms substantially overlap at L1-L2,
      and the 93.3% anatomical overlap means practitioners are
      accessing the same neuroanatomical substrates
    → The distinction is REAL but NARROWER than either profession
      typically acknowledges
    → = "Different entry point, same building" — DN enters through
      the L1 trigger point door; acupuncture enters through
      multiple doors simultaneously

### §8.1 — Sources of Individual Variation

```
🟡 WHY PATIENTS RESPOND DIFFERENTLY TO DN:

  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ① TRIGGER POINT PALPATION RELIABILITY:
  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

    The FIRST source of variation is DIAGNOSTIC:
    → Clinicians must FIND the trigger point by palpation

  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

      muscle guarding → harder to elicit clean LTR

  ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

### §8.3 — Contraindications

```
🟢 CONTRAINDICATIONS FOR DRY NEEDLING:

  ABSOLUTE:
    → Patient refusal / inability to consent
    → Needle phobia (severe, unmanageable)
    → Area of active infection or skin lesion
    → Unstable anticoagulation (INR > 3.0)
    → Lymphedematous tissue (limb at risk)
    → Over an implanted medical device (pacemaker, defibrillator)
    → Pregnancy: abdominal, lumbosacral, pelvic regions
      (limb/cervical: generally safe with caution)

  RELATIVE (proceed with caution):
    → Bleeding disorders or therapeutic anticoagulation (stable)
    → Immunocompromised patients
    → Needle-sensitive patients (consider SDN instead of DDN)
    → Near prosthetic implants or surgical hardware
    → Pediatric patients (limited evidence, parental consent)
    → Over areas with compromised skin integrity
    → Cervicothoracic region (pneumothorax risk → experienced
      practitioner only, angled insertion, appropriate depth)
    → First trimester pregnancy (extra caution, extremities only)

## §10 — OPEN QUESTIONS

```
🟡🔴 8 CRITICAL QUESTIONS FOR DRY NEEDLING RESEARCH:

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q1: IS LTR NECESSARY FOR THERAPEUTIC EFFECT? ⭐ CRITICAL
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    Hong 1994: LTR correlates with better outcomes
    Fernández-de-las-Peñas 2022: LTR may NOT be necessary
    Baldry: SDN (no LTR possible) effective in 90% of cases
    2025 SR: no clinically meaningful DDN vs SDN difference for neck
    Lateral epicondylitis 2024: LTR → better outcomes (supports)
    → If LTR is NOT necessary, the core DN rationale (ACh depletion
      at motor endplate) is NOT the primary mechanism
    → = Resolving this question determines whether DN is a
      SPECIFIC intervention (L1) or a non-specific needle
      stimulus with some trigger point-specific bonus
    TESTABLE: RCT comparing DDN with documented LTR vs DDN
    without LTR (matched needle time) for the SAME condition.
    PREDICTION FROM §5.3: LTR is ADDITIVE but not exclusive.

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q2: DEEP vs SUPERFICIAL — MECHANISM DIFFERENCE OR DOSE?
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    → Are DDN and SDN activating DIFFERENT mechanisms (L1 vs L2)?
    → Or is it the same mechanism at different DOSES?
    → Depth-response studies needed with mechanistic outcomes
      (not just clinical pain scales)
    → Needed: EMG, microdialysis, fMRI comparing DDN vs SDN

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q3: WHAT IS A TRIGGER POINT? (→ A08 §3.5 Q1)
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    4 competing models exist (Simons, Shah, Hocking, Strickland)
    → Is the trigger point primarily NMJ pathology (Simons, Hocking)?
    → Is it primarily a local biochemical lesion (Shah)?
    → Is it a PROTECTIVE adaptation (Strickland 2026)?
    → Or is it a peripheral sign of central sensitization?
    → The ANSWER determines which DN level (L1, L2, L3) is
      truly primary
    → = DN's theoretical foundation depends on resolving this
      question, which remains open after 80+ years of trigger point research

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q4: DN vs ACUPUNCTURE — SAME MECHANISM, DIFFERENT LABEL?
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    → 93.3% anatomical overlap + same needle = same L1-L2 biology?
    → If DNIC (L3) contributes significantly to DN analgesia,
      then the "specific" L1 component may be smaller than assumed
    → Head-to-head RCTs needed with MECHANISTIC outcomes
      (not just clinical comparison)
    → TESTABLE: DN pistoning vs acupuncture retention at the
      SAME points with microdialysis, fMRI, and opioid antagonist
    → Political barrier: neither profession incentivized to
      investigate (→ §9.3)

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q5: OPTIMAL DOSING (UNDERDEVELOPED)
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    → How many trigger points per session? (current: 1-6 by convention)
    → How many sessions? (current: 3-6 by convention)
    → What session frequency? (current: 1-3×/week by convention)
    → No dose-finding studies comparable to B01's ATC data
      (B01: more sessions β=0.11 p=0.0007; more needles +0.33 SD)
    → = DN dosing is based on CLINICAL TRADITION, not evidence

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q6: LONG-TERM EFFICACY — DOES DN PRODUCE LASTING CHANGE?
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    → Short-term pain relief: consistent evidence (§4.5)
    → Beyond 12 weeks: evidence weakens or disappears
    → Does DN-induced trigger point deactivation PERSIST?
    → Or do trigger points reactivate without addressing perpetuating factors?
    → B01 comparison: acupuncture shows ~90% benefit at 12 months
      (MacPherson 2017) — does DN match this?
    → = Long-term follow-up studies (>12 months) critically needed

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q7: PREDICTORS OF RESPONSE
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    → B01 has emerging prediction models (83-88% accuracy)
    → DN has NONE
    → Potential variables: pain catastrophizing, central
      sensitization inventory, number of elicitable LTRs,
      chronicity, psychological factors, genetics
    → = Without prediction, DN is applied uniformly to all
      patients, including likely non-responders
    → Genetic predictors: COMT (pain sensitivity), but no
      DN-specific genetic association studies exist

  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  Q8: FSN MECHANISM — HOW DOES SUBCUTANEOUS WORK?
  ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    → FSN does NOT enter muscle → NO LTR possible
    → FSN is positioned AWAY from the lesion site
    → Clinical effects reported for knee OA, epicondylalgia
    → Mechanism must operate through fascial continuity (A03)
      and/or mechanoreceptor-mediated pathways
    → = FSN is the most THEORETICALLY CHALLENGING DN variant
    → If FSN works WITHOUT accessing the trigger point, then
      the trigger point may not be the essential target
    → = Q8 connects to Q1 (LTR necessity) and Q3 (what IS a trigger point?)

  🟡 Questions: synthesized from evidence gaps identified in §4, §5, §9
  🔴 Several questions may be unanswerable with current methodology
```
