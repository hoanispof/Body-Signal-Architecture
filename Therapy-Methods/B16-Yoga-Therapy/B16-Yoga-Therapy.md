# Yoga Therapy (B16)

> **Central Finding:** Yoga therapy is the SECOND movement-based self-therapy in Track B
> (sister modality to B15 Tai Chi/Qigong), but with a fundamentally different therapeutic
> architecture. Where B15 provides UNIFORM broad activation through continuous flow at
> NARROW intensity (2-4 METs), B16 offers an **Adaptive Spectrum** — a family of
> structurally distinct sub-modalities (Yin, Restorative, Hatha, Iyengar, Vinyasa,
> Ashtanga, Bikram) spanning 2-8 METs, each emphasizing DIFFERENT Track A systems.
> This makes yoga the ONLY therapy in Track B that can be CONFIGURED to target specific
> physiological pathways by selecting different styles. The clinical profile reflects
> this: STRONGEST evidence for depression (SMD -0.52, very consistent), blood pressure,
> and glycemic control — conditions amenable to configurable multi-system intervention —
> while chronic LBP evidence consistently shows yoga EQUIVALENT TO other exercise
> (Sherman 2011, Saper 2017, Cochrane 2022), challenging claims of yoga-specific
> superiority for musculoskeletal pain.

## §1 — DEFINITION & ORIGIN

### §1.1 — What Yoga Therapy Is

**Three-tier distinction:** 🟢

1. **General/traditional yoga**: A spiritual-philosophical path originally defined by
   Patanjali's 8 limbs (ashtanga). Asana (physical postures) was a MINOR preparatory
   component — its entire treatment is one sutra: *sthira sukham asanam* ("the posture
   should be steady and comfortable"). Goal: self-realization, not symptom resolution.

2. **Yoga as exercise/fitness**: Physical postures practiced for fitness, flexibility,
   stress relief in group classes. No individualized clinical assessment. No
   diagnosis-linked adaptation. This is what most Westerners mean by "yoga."

3. **Yoga therapy (clinical/medical yoga)**: A HEALTHCARE INTERVENTION. The therapist
   conducts individualized assessment, sets goals tied to a health condition, and adapts
   yoga techniques (asana, pranayama, meditation, lifestyle) as treatment. The client
   presents with a symptom or condition, not a wish to "learn yoga."

**IAYT definition** (International Association of Yoga Therapists): "Yoga therapy is the
professional application of the principles and practices of yoga to promote health and
well-being within a therapeutic relationship that includes personalized assessment, goal
setting, lifestyle management, and yoga practices for individuals or small groups." 🟢

**This file's scope:** Covers all three tiers as they appear in clinical research. Most
RCTs use group-class yoga (tier 2) rather than individualized yoga therapy (tier 3).
This distinction matters because clinical evidence may underestimate yoga therapy's
potential (individualized) while overestimating yoga-as-exercise's specificity
(standardized class protocol). 🟡

**Parallel to B15:** B15 (Tai Chi/Qigong) also spans practice-for-health to clinical
application, but without a formal professional distinction like IAYT. Both share the
paradigm shift from passive reception (B01-B11) to active self-regulation — see B15
§7.3 for detailed paradigm comparison.

### §1.2 — Historical Context

**Vedic and pre-classical period:** 🟢

- **Rigveda** (~1500-1200 BCE): earliest references to practices resembling yoga —
  ascetic disciplines (tapas), breath control, meditation. Yoga as philosophical
  concept, not physical practice.
- **Upanishads** (~800-200 BCE): yoga as means to liberation. Katha Upanishad uses
  "yoga" to mean disciplined control of the senses. No physical postures described.
- **Bhagavad Gita** (~200 BCE): three paths of yoga — Karma (action), Bhakti
  (devotion), Jnana (knowledge). Physical practice absent.

**Classical period:** 🟢

- **Patanjali's Yoga Sutras** (~200 BCE-400 CE): 196 aphorisms defining 8 limbs:
  Yama (ethics), Niyama (discipline), Asana (posture), Pranayama (breath regulation),
  Pratyahara (sense withdrawal), Dharana (concentration), Dhyana (meditation),
  Samadhi (absorption). Asana receives ONE sutra. Physical postures were a
  preparatory step toward meditation, not the practice's purpose.

**Hatha yoga emergence:** 🟢

- **Hatha Yoga Pradipika** (~15th century, Svātmārāma): FIRST text to elaborate
  physical postures as a developed category — 15 postures described, plus pranayama,
  mudra (gesture), bandha (energy lock). This is the historical PIVOT point where
  physical practice becomes central to yoga, not incidental.
- **Gheranda Samhita** and **Shiva Samhita**: companion Hatha texts expanding the
  physical-practice repertoire.

**Western transmission:** 🟢

- **Swami Vivekananda** (1893): address at World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago.
  Introduced yoga to the West as PHILOSOPHY/MEDITATION, not physical postures.
  The postural-fitness wave came decades later.
- **Tirumalai Krishnamacharya** (1888-1989): "Father of modern yoga." Based in
  Mysore. Synthesized Hatha postures, gymnastics-influenced sequencing, and
  therapeutic individualization. Believed yoga could prevent and treat disease.
  His direct students created most major modern lineages:
  - **K. Pattabhi Jois** (1915-2009) → Ashtanga Vinyasa Yoga
  - **B.K.S. Iyengar** (1918-2014) → Iyengar Yoga
  - **T.K.V. Desikachar** (1938-2016, Krishnamacharya's son) → Viniyoga
    (explicitly therapeutic, individualized — direct ancestor of modern yoga therapy)
  - **Indra Devi** (1899-2002) → brought yoga to US/Hollywood

**Modern professionalization:** 🟢

- **IAYT founded** (1989): by Larry Payne, PhD, and Richard Miller, PhD. First
  professional body for yoga therapy.
- **C-IAYT certification**: minimum 800 hours over 2 years. 5,600+ professionals
  in 50+ countries as of 2026.
- **VA Whole Health integration**: yoga integrated alongside tai chi, acupuncture,
  mindfulness under Complementary and Integrative Health (CIH). Trauma-sensitive
  yoga programs for PTSD and military sexual trauma.
- **Insurance coverage**: largely indirect/uneven. Practitioner liability insurance
  exists, but direct third-party reimbursement for yoga therapy sessions remains
  limited — a meaningful gap vs physical therapy or acupuncture.

### §1.3 — Yoga Styles Taxonomy

Major styles relevant to clinical research: 🟢

| Style | Pace/Intensity | Emphasis | Research Status |
|-------|---------------|----------|-----------------|
| Hatha | Slow-moderate, 2-4 METs | Generic umbrella; postures + breathing | MOST studied label in RCTs |
| Iyengar | Slow, precise, 2-4 METs | Alignment, props, therapeutic holds | MOST studied for MSK pain |
| Viniyoga | Slow, individualized, 2-4 METs | Therapeutic adaptation | Closest to yoga therapy |
| Vinyasa | Moderate-fast, 4-7 METs | Breath-linked flowing sequences | Under-studied mechanistically |
| Ashtanga | Fast, vigorous, 5-8 METs | Fixed sequences, athletic | Closest to aerobic exercise |
| Yin | Very slow, passive, 2-3 METs | Long holds (1-20 min), fascia targeting | Mechanistically distinct |
| Restorative | Passive, prop-supported, 1-2 METs | Deep rest, minimal exertion | Used in Bower 2014 |
| Kundalini | Variable, 2-5 METs | Breath + chanting + meditation | Simon 2021 JAMA Psychiatry |
| Bikram/Hot | Fixed 26-pose, 40°C room, 4-6 METs | Heat + fixed sequence | Physiologically distinct |

**Classification axes:** 🟡
- **Intensity**: Yin/Restorative (passive) ↔ Ashtanga/Power (vigorous)
- **Rigidity**: Fixed sequence (Ashtanga, Bikram) ↔ adaptive (Viniyoga, Iyengar)
- **Orientation**: Fitness (Power, Vinyasa) ↔ therapeutic (Iyengar, Viniyoga)
- **Environment**: Room-temperature (all others) ↔ heated (Bikram/Hot)

**Research skew:** Hatha is the most common style label in RCTs (35/312 RCTs in
bibliometric analysis; 119/312 did not specify style). Iyengar dominates MSK pain
research. Restorative was used in the landmark inflammation study (Bower 2014).
The mechanistic literature is heavily concentrated in slower/alignment styles —
claims about "yoga" mechanisms may not transfer cleanly to vigorous styles. 🟡

**Yin yoga origins:** Developed from Taoist yoga by Paulie Zink (late 1970s),
refined by Paul Grilley (studied anatomy/kinesiology, met Dr. Hiroshi Motoyama
who linked meridian theory to fascia science, 1990), and named by Sarah Powers.
Long-held (1-20 min) passive postures targeting connective tissue/fascia rather
than muscle — the most A03-dominant yoga style. 🟢

**Bibliometric context:** Cramer et al. 2014 catalogued 366 papers / 312 RCTs
(1975-2014) across 23 countries. Most-studied conditions: breast cancer, depression,
asthma, T2DM. Most-used components: postures (244 RCTs), breath control (232),
meditation (153). 🟢

### §1.4 — Cross-Cultural and Traditional Framework

**Sanskrit terminology:** 🟢
- **Asana** (आसन): physical posture
- **Pranayama** (प्राणायाम): breath regulation (prana = vital energy, ayama = extension)
- **Dhyana** (ध्यान): meditation/meditative absorption
- **Pratyahara** (प्रत्याहार): sense withdrawal
- **Dharana** (धारणा): concentration/focused attention
- **Samadhi** (समाधि): absorption/union — the goal of classical yoga

**Traditional energy framework:** 🔴 as mechanism explanation
- **Prana** (प्राण): vital energy/life force — functional analog of Chinese qi (气)
- **Nadis** (नाडी): energy channels (~72,000 traditionally described) — structural
  analog of meridians (经络). Three primary: Ida (left/lunar/parasympathetic),
  Pingala (right/solar/sympathetic), Sushumna (central channel along spine)
- **Chakras** (चक्र): seven primary energy centers along the spine — described as
  intersection points of nadis. Structural analog of acupoints (穴位)

**Scientific assessment:** No confirmed anatomical or physiological basis for
chakras as discrete energy centers or nadis as energy channels. Mainstream science
treats the system as unverified pre-scientific models — same evidentiary status
as meridians in TCM (see A09 §4 for comprehensive analysis). The ida/pingala
mapping to parasympathetic/sympathetic has face validity as a phenomenological
observation but is not evidence of literal energy channels. 🔴

**Cross-cultural names:** Yoga (English/international), योग (Sanskrit/Hindi),
Yog (Marathi/Gujarati), யோகா (Tamil). Unlike B15's Chinese-specific origin,
yoga has spread so widely that the Sanskrit term is now universally used.

## §2 — STIMULATION TYPE

### §2.1 — Self-Generated Composite Stimulus (B16 Variant)

B16 shares the self-generated composite stimulus paradigm with B15 (see B15 §2)
but with STRUCTURALLY DIFFERENT components: 🟡

```
B15 vs B16 STIMULUS COMPARISON:

  B15 (Tai Chi/Qigong)              B16 (Yoga Therapy)
  ─────────────────────             ──────────────────────
  ① CONTINUOUS flow                 ① HELD POSTURES (asanas)
     (no stops between postures)       (sustained isometric + stretch)
  ② NATURAL breathing               ② STRUCTURED pranayama
     (coordinated with movement)       (dedicated breathing exercises)
  ③ MOVING meditation               ③ SEATED/LYING meditation
     (attention follows body)          (often separate from movement)
  ④ BALANCE-dominant                ④ FLEXIBILITY-dominant
     (constant weight shifting)        (end-range holds)
  ⑤ NARROW intensity (2-4 METs)     ⑤ WIDE intensity (2-8 METs)
  ⑥ UNIFORM practice                ⑥ CONFIGURABLE styles
     (all styles similar intensity)    (Yin ≠ Ashtanga)
```

### §2.2 — Component Parameters

**Component ① — Asana (postures):** 🟢

| Parameter | Value | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Modality | Self-generated mechanical (isometric contraction + stretch) | No external force |
| Duration per pose | 5 breaths to 20 min | Style-dependent: Vinyasa 5 breaths, Yin 1-20 min |
| Session duration | 60-90 min (typical class) | Range: 20 min (brief) to 120 min (Ashtanga primary) |
| Intensity | 2-8 METs | Restorative ~2, Hatha ~3, Vinyasa ~5, Ashtanga ~7-8 |
| Force type | Isometric + eccentric + stretch | Gravity-loaded (body weight only) |
| Primary tissue targets | Muscle, fascia, tendon, joint capsule | Style-dependent emphasis |

**Component ② — Pranayama (breath regulation):** 🟢

| Technique | Rate | Pattern | Autonomic Direction |
|-----------|------|---------|-------------------|
| Ujjayi (ocean breath) | 4-8/min | Constricted glottis, audible | Parasympathetic ↑ |
| Nadi Shodhana (alternate nostril) | 4-6/min | L/R alternation | Parasympathetic ↑ |
| Bhramari (humming bee) | 3-5/min | Humming exhalation | Parasympathetic ↑ (laryngeal vagal?) |
| 4:7:8 breathing | ~3/min | Extended exhalation | Strong parasympathetic ↑ |
| Kapalabhati (skull shining) | 60-120/min | Rapid forceful exhalation | Sympathetic ↑ |
| Bhastrika (bellows) | 30-60/min | Rapid forceful in + out | Sympathetic ↑ |

Pranayama is STRUCTURALLY DISTINCT from B15's breathing:
- B15: breathing is integrated INTO movement (natural coordination)
- B16: pranayama is a DEDICATED practice, often performed separately from asana
- B16 includes BOTH calming (slow) AND activating (rapid) techniques
- B16's pranayama range spans from ~3/min (4:7:8) to 120/min (Kapalabhati)

The calming techniques operate through the same Zone D Bridge mechanism as B15
(A02 §3.7: slow breathing → vagal tone → parasympathetic shift). The activating
techniques (Kapalabhati, Bhastrika) represent a UNIQUE feature — no other Track B
therapy deliberately induces SYMPATHETIC activation as a therapeutic tool. 🟡

**Component ③ — Dhyana (meditation/mindfulness):** 🟡

- Often a SEPARATE component of yoga practice, not embedded in movement (contrast B15)
- Body scan (Yoga Nidra), focused attention (trataka), mantra meditation
- Savasana (corpse pose): systematic relaxation, typically 5-15 min at session end
- Meditation component is what DISTINGUISHES yoga therapy from exercise-only yoga

**Component ④ — Philosophical/lifestyle framework:** 🟡

Yama (ethical restraints) + Niyama (observances) + lifestyle guidance = components
that influence behavior BETWEEN practice sessions. This is analogous to the
self-efficacy cascade in B15 (§5.2.6) but MORE structured and explicit.

## §3 — APPLICATION POINTS & AREAS

### §3.1 — No Point Specificity

Like B15, B16 has NO point specificity. There are no specific application points
(no acupoints, no trigger points, no dermatome-based targeting). The "therapeutic
target" is the WHOLE BODY through movement, breathing, and attention. 🟢

### §3.2 — Region-Dependent Loading by Style

Although B16 lacks point specificity, different STYLES and POSES create
disproportionate loading on specific body regions: 🟡

```
POSE CATEGORIES → REGIONAL EMPHASIS:

  Standing poses (Warrior I/II/III, Triangle, Chair):
    → Lower limbs PRIMARY (quads, hamstrings, calves)
    → Core STRONG (stability demand)
    → Balance challenge (proprioceptive training)

  Forward folds (Uttanasana, Paschimottanasana):
    → Posterior chain (hamstrings, erector spinae, TLF)
    → Fascial Superficial Back Line (Myers, A03 §1.3)

  Backbends (Cobra, Upward Dog, Bridge, Wheel):
    → Anterior chain (hip flexors, abdominals, chest)
    → Spinal extension → intervertebral disc nutrition
    → Fascial Superficial Front Line

  Twists (Revolved Triangle, Seated Twist):
    → Spinal rotation → intervertebral disc hydration
    → Fascial Spiral Line (A03 §1.3)
    → Visceral massage (traditional claim, limited evidence)

  Inversions (Headstand, Shoulderstand, Legs-up-the-wall):
    → Altered gravitational hemodynamics
    → Venous/lymphatic return facilitation
    → Baroreceptor stimulation (A02)

  Hip openers (Pigeon, Bound Angle):
    → Deep hip fascia and joint capsule
    → Psoas complex (often hypertonic in chronic LBP)

  Balance poses (Tree, Eagle, Warrior III):
    → Proprioceptive training (closest to B15's emphasis)
    → Ankle/foot stabilizer strengthening
```

### §3.3 — Yin Yoga: Fascial Targeting

Yin yoga represents the MOST tissue-specific yoga approach: 🟡

Long-held passive postures (1-20 min) at end range, targeting connective tissue
(fascia, ligaments, joint capsules) rather than contractile muscle. Rationale:
fascia is viscoelastic and responds to SLOW sustained loading (creep, stress
relaxation) differently from rapid stretch. Muscle relaxation is deliberately
sought so that mechanical load transfers to deeper connective tissue layers.

This is mechanistically aligned with A03's fascial elongation model:
- Sustained load → viscoelastic creep → fascial lengthening
- HA (hyaluronan) metabolism → improved fascial hydration
- Collagen fiber realignment along lines of force (long-term)
- Fibroblast mechanotransduction (Rho/Rac, A03 §1.4)

Yin yoga is the MOST A03-dominant yoga style and potentially the most
A03-dominant therapy in all of Track B (competing with B04 deep tissue massage
for this distinction). 🟡

## §4 — DOCUMENTED CLINICAL EFFECTS

### §4.1 — Evidence Landscape

Wang et al. 2025 umbrella review: 51 SRs (34 meta-analyses), 579 RCTs,
28,403 participants. The most comprehensive synthesis of yoga's clinical
evidence as of 2026. 🟢

Cramer et al. 2014 bibliometric analysis: 312 RCTs (1975-2014), 22,548
participants, 23 countries. 🟢

### §4.2 — Strongest Evidence (🟢🟢 Multiple Consistent SRs)

**Depression:** 🟢🟢
- Wang 2025 umbrella: 10 SRs (9 meta-analyzed), SMD -0.52 (95% CI -0.62, -0.41),
  I²=6% (VERY consistent — lowest heterogeneity of any outcome).
- Cramer 2013: 12 RCTs, n=619, SMD -0.35 vs no-treatment controls.
- B16's STRONGEST clinical signal. Depression shows the most CONSISTENT benefit
  across diverse yoga styles, populations, and study designs.
- Mechanism candidates: GABA hypothesis (Streeter, §5.2.4), vagal tone (§5.2.2),
  self-efficacy (§5.2.6), HPA axis modulation (§5.2.4).

**Blood pressure / hypertension:** 🟢🟢
- Wang 2025 umbrella: STRONG evidence, systolic SMD -0.48, diastolic SMD -0.39.
- 2024-2025 systematic review (30 RCTs, n=2,283): vs waitlist systolic -7.95 mmHg, diastolic
  -4.93 mmHg; vs active control -4.16/-1.88 mmHg (smaller).
- VA 2025 lists HIGH certainty for yoga/tai chi + hypertension.
- Mechanism: autonomic rebalancing (parasympathetic ↑, sympathetic ↓) via
  breathing practices.

**Blood glucose / Type 2 Diabetes:** 🟢🟢
- Wang 2025 umbrella: STRONG evidence, FBG SMD -1.24, PPBS SMD -0.70,
  HbA1c SMD -0.72.
- Multiple meta-analyses converge: 3-month protocols reduce HbA1c ~0.55-0.64
  percentage points, improve insulin resistance (HOMA-IR).
- One of the more CONSISTENT physiological-outcome signals for yoga.
- Mechanism candidates: autonomic modulation (improved insulin sensitivity via
  parasympathetic dominance), exercise physiology (muscle glucose uptake),
  cortisol reduction (stress-induced insulin resistance ↓).

**Cancer-related fatigue:** 🟢🟢
- Wang 2025 umbrella: STRONG evidence, SMD -0.40, I²=44%.
- Breast cancer meta-analysis (18 studies): fatigue SMD -0.51, anxiety SMD -0.93,
  depression SMD -1.23, QoL MD -11.20.
- Dose-response identified: optimal ~220 min/week.
- Key researcher: Lorenzo Cohen, MD Anderson Cancer Center.
- Umbrella review of yoga in cancer care: CONVINCING for depression in breast
  cancer; only WEAK for QoL, fatigue, anxiety, sleep.

### §4.3 — Strong Evidence (🟢 Multiple RCTs)

**Chronic low back pain:** 🟢 (with critical caveat)
- Cochrane 2022 (Wieland): yoga slightly better than no exercise for back
  function and pain at 3 months, but the difference "is likely too small to
  be clinically important to the patient."
- Sherman 2011: yoga > self-care book, but **NOT superior to generic stretching**.
- Saper 2017 (Ann Intern Med): yoga NONINFERIOR to physical therapy. n=320,
  predominantly low-income/racially diverse. Both reduced analgesic use ~50-70%.
- Tilbrook 2011 (Ann Intern Med): yoga > usual care for back function at 3/6/12
  months, but back PAIN scores similar between groups.
- Williams 2009 (Spine): Iyengar yoga > usual care for pain/disability/depression.
- **CRITICAL CAVEAT: Yoga ≈ exercise for LBP.** Consistently equivalent to
  generic exercise, stretching, or PT — not superior. The clinical guidelines
  (ACP, NICE, VA/DoD) list yoga as ONE of many first-line exercise options,
  not a uniquely effective one.

**Neck pain:** 🟢
- Cramer 2017 (3 RCTs, n=188): short-term benefit vs usual care.
- 2019 meta-analysis (10 trials, n=686): pain SMD -1.13, disability SMD -0.92,
  plus ROM improvements.
- One of the STRONGER MSK signals (vs usual care, not vs exercise).

**PTSD:** 🟢
- van der Kolk 2014 (J Clin Psychiatry): foundational trauma-sensitive yoga
  (TCTSY) RCT. 10-week program → significant PTSD symptom reduction, largest
  effects in most treatment-resistant participants.
- 2024 meta-analysis (20 RCTs): PTSD SMD -0.51 (95% CI -0.68, -0.35).
- Earlier 2018 meta-analysis (19 RCTs, n=1,173): ES -0.39.
- VA trauma-sensitive yoga programs for PTSD and military sexual trauma.

### §4.4 — Moderate Evidence (🟡)

**Anxiety:** 🟡
- Wang 2025 umbrella: MODERATE evidence, SMD -0.55, but I²=60% (high).
- Cramer 2018: 8 RCTs, n=319, SMD -0.43 vs no-treatment.
- **CRITICAL:** No significant effect for DSM-diagnosed anxiety disorders —
  benefit mainly in people with ELEVATED symptoms, not clinical diagnosis.
- Simon 2021 (JAMA Psychiatry, n=226): Kundalini yoga vs CBT vs stress education
  for GAD. Both yoga and CBT > stress education, but **CBT OUTPERFORMED yoga**.
  Supports CBT remaining first-line for diagnosed GAD.

**Knee osteoarthritis:** 🟡
- 2024 meta-analysis (8 RCTs, n=756): pain SMD -0.92, stiffness SMD -0.51,
  function SMD -0.53. No improvement in ADLs/QoL.
- "Weak recommendation" due to low methodological quality.
- YOGA Trial (Australia, 2024-2025): first head-to-head yoga vs strengthening
  exercise for knee OA.

**Insomnia/sleep:** 🟡
- Overall PSQI SMD -0.327 for sleep quality.
- Women specifically (16 RCTs): PSQI SMD -0.54, but Insomnia Severity Index
  (3 RCTs): NO effect (SMD -0.13, ns).
- Elderly: significant positive effect.
- Breast cancer patients (7 RCTs): NO significant effect on PSQI.
- Yoga improves PERCEIVED sleep quality more than diagnosed insomnia severity.

**Fibromyalgia:** 🟡
- Carson 2010 ("Yoga of Awareness" pilot, n=53): 31.4% improvement in FIQ,
  reduced pain/fatigue/pain catastrophizing, improved coping/acceptance.
- Thin evidence base — promising pilot data, few large RCTs.

**COPD/Asthma:** 🟡
- Cramer 2019: improved pulmonary function/exercise capacity in some trials.
- Pranayama-specific RCTs for asthma (RCT comparing Buteyko vs pranayama
  vs relaxation: pranayama > relaxation for asthma control).
- Pranayama for COPD exercise tolerance: positive.
- Evidence base smaller/lower quality than for depression or LBP.

### §4.5 — Weak Evidence (🟡🔴)

**Multiple sclerosis:** 🟡🔴
- Cramer 2014 (7 RCTs, n=670, later 10 RCTs/n=693): short-term benefit for
  fatigue and mood only — NOT for QoL, muscle function, or cognition.
- Effects "not robust against bias."
- **No advantage of yoga over exercise** in any outcome.

**Rheumatoid arthritis:** 🟡🔴
- Cramer: positive but limited-quality evidence.

**General pain (non-specific):** 🟡🔴
- Wang 2025 umbrella: SMD -0.72 but I²=75% (unstable, unreliable).

### §4.6 — Clinical Profile Summary

```
B16 CLINICAL PROFILE — CONFIGURABLE SPECTRUM:

  STRONGEST (🟢🟢, STRONG evidence, consistent):
    Depression (SMD -0.52, I²=6%)
    Blood pressure (SMD -0.48/-0.39)
    Glycemic control / T2DM (FBG SMD -1.24, HbA1c SMD -0.72)
    Cancer-related fatigue (SMD -0.40)

  STRONG (🟢, multiple RCTs):
    Chronic LBP (but ≈ exercise, not superior)
    Neck pain (vs usual care, not vs exercise)
    PTSD (SMD -0.51, trauma-sensitive yoga)

  MODERATE (🟡):
    Anxiety (subclinical > clinical; CBT > yoga for GAD)
    Knee OA, insomnia/sleep, fibromyalgia, COPD/asthma

  WEAK (🟡🔴):
    MS, RA, general pain (high heterogeneity)

  THE CRITICAL PATTERN:
  1. Depression, NOT pain, is yoga's strongest evidence — different from
     most Track B therapies where pain dominance is expected.
  2. Yoga ≈ exercise for MSK conditions. Rarely outperforms active comparators.
     This is the most consistent and important finding across the literature.
  3. Conditions with autonomic/neuroendocrine components (depression, BP,
     T2DM) show STRONGER signal than pure MSK conditions — consistent
     with yoga's pranayama/meditation components adding value BEYOND
     exercise physiology alone.
```

## §5 — PROPOSED MECHANISMS

### §5.1 — Traditional Explanation (Recorded as Data) 🔴

**Classical yoga framework:**

1. **Prana flow**: Yoga practices (asana, pranayama) guide prana (vital energy)
   through nadis (energy channels). Blockages in nadis → disease; free flow →
   health. Asana "opens" physical pathways; pranayama "purifies" energy channels.

2. **Chakra activation**: Seven primary chakras (root to crown) along the spine
   correspond to physical, emotional, and spiritual domains. Specific asanas and
   pranayama techniques are said to activate specific chakras. Each chakra
   corresponds to specific organs, glands, and psychological functions.

3. **Kosha model (5 sheaths)**: Human being consists of 5 nested layers —
   Annamaya (physical), Pranamaya (energy), Manomaya (mental), Vijnanamaya
   (wisdom), Anandamaya (bliss). Yoga therapy addresses all 5 layers, unlike
   conventional medicine (physical only). This model functions as a
   biopsychosocial-spiritual framework.

4. **Guna theory**: Three qualities (sattva/rajas/tamas) govern all phenomena.
   Disease = imbalance of gunas. Yoga practice cultivates sattva (clarity/harmony).

**Assessment:** These frameworks organize practice and instruction effectively.
They represent sophisticated phenomenological observations analogous to TCM's
qi/meridian framework (B15 §5.1). The kosha model parallels Western
biopsychosocial frameworks. However, none constitute mechanism explanations
testable by scientific method. Prana/nadi/chakra systems have no confirmed
anatomical or physiological correlates beyond known structures (see A09 §4). 🔴

### §5.2 — Modern Research-Based Mechanisms

#### §5.2.1 — Level 1: Musculoskeletal (Posture-Based Loading) 🟢

**Held postures → static stretch + isometric loading:**

Yoga asanas create SUSTAINED mechanical loading through body-weight resistance
against gravity. Unlike B15's continuous flow (dynamic loading), B16's held
postures produce STATIC loading — isometric muscle contraction + fascial stretch
simultaneously. 🟢

Mechanism chain for held postures:
```
Asana hold (30s - 20min)
  → Isometric muscle contraction (gravity resistance)
    → Motor unit recruitment → strength maintenance
    → Gamma loop engagement (sustained spindle activation)
  → Static stretch of antagonist muscles
    → Viscoelastic creep in fascia/tendons
    → Stretch tolerance increase (neural, not structural — short-term)
  → Joint capsule loading
    → Joint receptor activation (Freeman-Wyke Types I-III, A08 §1.4)
    → Improved proprioceptive acuity
  → Sustained afferent input
    → Gate control (Aβ mechanoreceptors, A01 §2.1)
    → Descending modulation engagement (over time)
```

**Fascial elongation (Yin yoga mechanism):** 🟡

Long-held passive postures (1-20 min) at end range target connective tissue
through viscoelastic creep. Fascia researcher Robert Schleip: fascia "adapts to
regular stretching-loading demands — it changes its length, strength, and alignment.
If tensile forces are frequently repeated, the collagenous fibers are strengthened
and become more injury resistant."

Dense collagen-rich tissues (tendons, ligaments, fascial sheets) require LONGER
time-under-tension (~90-120 seconds minimum) for measurable lengthening/remodeling
compared to the 30-60 second holds of standard static stretching. Yin yoga's
extended holds are designed to exceed this threshold. This connects to A03 §1.2
(fascial viscoelasticity) and A03 §4.2 (yoga/tai chi fascial training effects).

**Joint moments of force:** 🟡

During asana holds, gravity creates rotational forces at joints that muscles and
ligaments must resist. Each pose creates a specific pattern of joint loading that
can be quantified biomechanically (PMC3814077: physical demand profiles of Hatha
yoga postures in older adults).

**Proprioceptive training in balance poses:** 🟢

Balance poses (Tree/Vrksasana, Warrior III/Virabhadrasana III, Eagle/Garudasana)
challenge the proprioceptive system through:
- Reduced base of support (single-leg stance)
- Head/body position changes relative to gravity
- Eyes-closed variants (removing visual compensation)
- Vestibular-proprioceptive-visual integration demand

Balance effect is REAL but LESS sustained than B15's continuous weight-shifting
pattern. B15 maintains constant balance challenge throughout the entire practice
(45-60 min). B16 challenges balance in discrete poses separated by non-balance
poses. This may explain why B15 outperforms B16 for fall prevention: quantity
and continuity of proprioceptive challenge matters. 🟡

#### §5.2.2 — Level 2: Autonomic Regulation (Pranayama → Vagal Tone) 🟢

**Pranayama as primary ANS modulator:**

Pranayama directly accesses the autonomic nervous system through the Zone D
Bridge mechanism (A02 §3.7). Unlike B15's breathing (natural coordination with
movement), B16's pranayama is a DEDICATED, STRUCTURED practice with specific
techniques for specific autonomic effects. 🟢

**Slow pranayama → parasympathetic activation:**

Mechanism chain (same as B15 §5.2.2, but with MORE explicit targeting):
```
Slow breathing (4-6/min, extended exhalation)
  → Prolonged vagal brake application
    → Increased parasympathetic tone
      → ↓HR, ↑HRV, ↓BP
        → Downstream: ↓cortisol, ↑immune, ↑sleep
```

Specific techniques and evidence:
- **Nadi Shodhana** (alternate nostril): RCT found 15 min reduced perceived stress,
  HR, and BP. Theory: differential hemispheric/autonomic stimulation (🟡).
- **Bhramari** (humming bee): rapid anxiety reduction. Humming vibration may
  stimulate laryngeal vagal afferents (🔴 specific pathway unconfirmed).
- **Extended exhalation** (4:7:8 pattern): longer exhalation → more time with
  vagal brake ON → training effect on vagal tone over time (🟢).

**Activating pranayama — UNIQUE to B16:** 🟡

Kapalabhati (rapid forceful exhalation, 60-120/min) and Bhastrika (rapid
forceful in + out, 30-60/min) produce the OPPOSITE autonomic direction:
increased LF power / decreased HF power on HRV = increased SYMPATHETIC
activation. 🟢

This is UNIQUE in Track B. No other therapy deliberately induces sympathetic
activation as a therapeutic tool. The theoretical rationale: autonomic
FLEXIBILITY training — cycling between parasympathetic (slow pranayama) and
sympathetic (rapid pranayama) states → improved autonomic range/recovery.
Analogous to exercise physiology: aerobic training improves BOTH exercise
capacity AND recovery. 🟡

**HRV evidence:** 🟡
- Meta-analysis 1 (~17 RCTs): normalized LF (g=-0.39), normalized HF (g=0.37),
  LF/HF ratio (g=-0.58) — significant favorable effects.
- Meta-analysis 2 (14 trials): only 2 methodologically acceptable, roughly half
  showing favorable effects — evidence quality concern.
- One study: 2×30 min/week increased HF, decreased LF and LF/HF ratio.
- Genuine MIXED evidence — meta-analyses disagree on magnitude.

**Brown & Gerbarg 2005:** 🟢
Foundational citation. Published two papers on Sudarshan Kriya Yoga breathing —
neurophysiologic model + clinical applications for stress/anxiety/depression.
Framework: breath-based "bottom-up" regulation — slow/coherent breathing patterns
influence musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, and nervous-system function, modulating
HPA axis and sympathetic activity. Extended toward Polyvagal Theory in subsequent
work (PMC5835127, 2018). A02 already cites Brown & Gerbarg (lines 974, 1427, 1713).

#### §5.2.3 — Level 3: Neuroimmune (Transcriptional Anti-Inflammatory) 🟢🟡

**Bower et al. 2014 — landmark finding:** 🟢

Psychoneuroendocrinology. 12-week restorative Iyengar yoga RCT in fatigued
breast cancer survivors. KEY FINDINGS:

- **No significant change** in CRP, IL-6, IL-1, or diurnal cortisol
  (standard circulating inflammatory markers were FLAT)
- **BUT** transcriptional/molecular-level changes:
  - Reduced NF-κB activity (pro-inflammatory transcription factor)
  - Increased glucocorticoid receptor (GR) activity (anti-inflammatory)
  - Reduced CREB-family transcription factor activity
- sTNF-RII (marker of TNF activity) stable in yoga group while it INCREASED
  in health-education control → yoga PREVENTED inflammatory increase rather
  than actively lowering inflammation

**Implication for B16's anti-inflammatory mechanism:** 🟡

Yoga's anti-inflammatory effect operates at a DIFFERENT LEVEL than exercise's
myokine pathway (A05 §4.4). Exercise: muscle contraction → IL-6 classic signaling
→ ↓TNF-α. Yoga (at least restorative styles): stress-axis/transcriptional
modulation (NF-κB↓, GR↑) → gene expression regulation.

This difference may explain why yoga shows anti-inflammatory effects even at
LOW intensity (restorative, 1-2 METs) where myokine production would be minimal —
the autonomic/stress-axis pathway operates independently of exercise intensity. 🟡

**Broader evidence:** 🟢
Systematic review (PMC11068076): majority of studies (24) reported favorable
outcomes for inflammatory markers regardless of yoga type, condition, or
duration. Yoga reduced pro-inflammatory and increased anti-inflammatory
cytokines in at least one industrial-worker RCT.

**B16 vs B15 anti-inflammatory comparison:** 🟡
- B15: dual pathway (myokine from movement + vagal from breathing) — §5.2.3
- B16: potentially TRIPLE pathway:
  (1) Myokine (from vigorous styles: Vinyasa, Ashtanga)
  (2) Vagal anti-inflammatory (from pranayama)
  (3) Transcriptional regulation (from restorative/meditation, Bower 2014)
  The relative contribution of each pathway likely depends on STYLE —
  vigorous styles → more myokine; gentle styles → more transcriptional.

#### §5.2.4 — Level 4: Neuroendocrine (GABA Hypothesis + Cortisol + eCB) 🟡

**GABA hypothesis — Streeter et al.:** 🟡

The most yoga-SPECIFIC mechanism hypothesis in the entire Track B literature.
Not shared with B15 or any other therapy.

- **Streeter 2007** (Boston University, pilot): experienced yoga practitioners
  showed 27% increase in brain GABA levels (MRS) after a single yoga session.
  No significant change in non-practitioner comparison group.
- **Streeter 2010** (J Altern Complement Med, RCT): yoga vs metabolically-matched
  walking, 3×/week for 12 weeks. Yoga group: greater mood improvement AND
  greater anxiety reduction than walking group. Designed specifically to rule
  out the confound that GABA increases were just from generic exercise.
- **Streeter 2012** (clinical extension): MDD patients — lower baseline GABA,
  which INCREASED over 12-week yoga intervention as symptoms declined.
  Normalization pattern: larger effect in more deficient population.
- **Streeter 2012** (Medical Hypotheses, theoretical): "Vagal-GABA theory" —
  chronic stress → ANS imbalance (↓PNS/↑SNS) + GABA-system underactivity +
  increased allostatic load. Yoga corrects PNS/GABA underactivity partly
  via vagal nerve stimulation, reducing allostatic load.

**Assessment:** The Vagal-GABA theory is mechanistically elegant and supported
by pilot data, but it is explicitly a HYPOTHESIS — the 2012 paper is titled
"Medical Hypotheses." Sample sizes are small (n < 30 per group). The causal
chain (yoga → vagus → GABA → mood) needs replication and larger studies.
It is PLAUSIBLE that yoga's depression efficacy (§4.2, strongest evidence)
relates to GABA normalization, but this remains 🟡 synthesis, not 🟢 confirmed.

**Cortisol:** 🟡 (mixed)
- Systematic review: "strong evidence that yoga interventions improve cortisol
  levels." But Bower 2014 found NO significant change in diurnal cortisol.
- Network meta-analysis: yoga shows dose-response curve peaking at moderate load
  (~300 MET-weeks), similar to continuous aerobic exercise — not uniquely potent
  vs other exercise for cortisol.
- B16 cortisol findings are GENUINELY MIXED, not uniformly positive.
  Same caveat as B03/B15: Moyer qualification applies (between-group effects
  smaller than pre-post; see RC#2).

**Endocannabinoid system:** 🟡
- 4-day intensive Isha Yoga retreat: AEA, 2-AG, 1-AG, and BDNF increased >70%,
  correlating with improved well-being sustained at 1-month follow-up.
- BUT routine yoga/stretching sessions did NOT produce measurable eCB changes.
- Dose/intensity matters: intensive multi-day retreats ≠ weekly studio classes.
- Vigorous styles (Ashtanga, Power) at 5-8 METs likely operate within the eCB
  release window (A06 §4.3), while gentle styles may fall below the threshold.

**β-endorphin:** 🟡
Expected from sustained moderate-intensity exercise in vigorous styles.
Less relevant for gentle/restorative styles. Same considerations as B15 §5.2.4.

#### §5.2.5 — Level 5: Neuroplasticity (Brain Structure and Function) 🟡

**Grey matter changes:**
- Review of 34 neuroimaging studies (MRI/PET/SPECT): consistent findings of
  increased grey matter volume in INSULA and HIPPOCAMPUS, increased prefrontal
  cortical activation. 🟡
- Insula = primary interoceptive cortex (Craig 2002). Increased volume →
  enhanced body awareness capacity. Same finding as B15 (Tao 2017).
- Hippocampus: memory and spatial processing. Consistent with B15 finding.
- Prefrontal cortex: attention and executive control. Thicker in older
  meditation practitioners — interpreted as offsetting age-related cortical
  thinning (Lazar-type studies).

**Functional connectivity:**
- Yoga Nidra: reduced DMN connectivity during practice + bilateral thalamic
  activation absent in non-meditator controls. 🟡
- Long-term Kripalu yoga/Vipassana: greater widespread functional connectivity
  of caudate.
- Elderly long-term practitioners: greater anteroposterior DMN connectivity.
- Similar pattern to B15 (Tao 2017, 2019 Nature Sci Rep) — mind-body practices
  appear to enhance functional network integration.

**Movement component vs meditation-only:** 🔴

This is an UNDEREXPLORED question. One study found activation in motor-planning
regions during yoga-type practice in both meditator and novice groups, indicating
the physical/motor-planning component is neurally DISTINCT and ADDITIVE to
seated meditation. But NO clean head-to-head "yoga (movement+breath) vs
meditation-only" neuroimaging RCT was found. This is probably the single
biggest unanswered mechanistic question in the yoga therapy literature.

**Limitation:** Most neuroimaging studies are SMALL (n < 30), many cross-sectional
(self-selection bias: people with certain brain characteristics may choose yoga).
Same limitation as B15 §5.2.5. 🟡

#### §5.2.6 — Level 6: Psychosocial (Self-Efficacy + Body Awareness + Mindfulness) 🟢🟡

**Self-efficacy — BEST-QUANTIFIED mechanism:** 🟢

Causal mediation analysis of PT and yoga for chronic LBP (PMC9510936):
- **Self-efficacy mediated 36% of the effect on disability**
- Sleep-disturbance mediated 18%
- Best combination of all mediators explained 61% of total effect

This is one of the most rigorously QUANTIFIED mechanistic findings in the
entire yoga and Track B literature. It provides concrete evidence that yoga's
benefit for LBP is substantially (>1/3) mediated through PSYCHOLOGICAL pathways
(confidence in ability to manage pain/function despite pain), not purely through
physical mechanisms. 🟢

B16 shares the self-efficacy cascade with B15 (§5.2.6) — both involve the
patient as active agent → mastery experience → confidence → positive feedback
loop. The 36% mediation figure provides the QUANTIFICATION that was theoretical
in B15.

**Body awareness / interoception:** 🟡

Scoping review (PMC10768869): "interoceptive techniques including attending to
and acceptance of bodily sensations" = key candidate mechanism for mind-body
practices in chronic pain. Pilot study: "Interoception-Based Yoga for Chronic
Pain" (2025) — emerging research.

Acute effect: improved Interoceptive Accuracy (heartbeat counting task) after
a single yoga class in healthy controls. 🟡

This connects directly to Human-Predictive-Drive's interoception framework (Keystone #17):
yoga improves interoceptive accuracy → enhanced body signal detection →
better self-regulation capacity. The B16 → interoception pathway is MORE
explicit than B15's, because yoga includes DEDICATED body-scan practices
(Yoga Nidra, savasana) that systematically train interoceptive attention. 🟡

**Mindfulness/acceptance-based mechanisms:** 🟡

Multiple reviews: yoga's psychological benefit operates through rhythmic
breathing + mindfulness + meditation → improved pain catastrophizing, pain
self-efficacy, disability. Some studies found no change in pain INTENSITY
despite improved COPING/FUNCTION — suggesting psychological mechanisms can
operate somewhat independently of nociceptive-level pain reduction.

This parallels Human-Predictive-Drive's Evaluative Gates: yoga changes the EVALUATIVE context
around pain (catastrophizing ↓, acceptance ↑) even when the sensory signal
remains unchanged.

### §7.2 — B16 vs Exercise (The Central Question)

The MOST IMPORTANT comparison for B16. Equivalent to B15's §7.2 but with MORE
head-to-head data: 🟢

**Evidence suggesting yoga ≈ exercise (NOT superior):**
- Sherman 2011: yoga NOT superior to generic stretching for LBP
- Saper 2017: yoga NONINFERIOR to physical therapy for LBP
- Cochrane 2022: yoga "similar effects to other exercise" for LBP
- MS reviews: no advantage over exercise in any outcome
- Wang 2025 umbrella: pain I²=75% (unstable)
- Cortisol: similar dose-response to continuous aerobic exercise

**Evidence suggesting yoga > exercise (yoga-specific benefit):**
- Streeter 2010: yoga > metabolically-matched walking for mood/anxiety/GABA
- Bower 2014: transcriptional anti-inflammatory at LOW intensity (exercise
  myokine pathway requires higher intensity)
- Depression: SMD -0.52 with very low heterogeneity (I²=6%) — the MOST
  consistent finding in yoga research
- PTSD: van der Kolk 2014 — trauma-sensitive yoga for treatment-resistant
  patients (exercise alone does not have this evidence base for PTSD)

**Synthesis:** 🟡
For MUSCULOSKELETAL conditions (LBP, neck pain, OA), yoga ≈ exercise —
benefits likely arise from EXERCISE-EQUIVALENT mechanisms (movement,
social context, self-efficacy, attention) available in any comparable
group physical activity.

For AUTONOMIC/NEUROENDOCRINE conditions (depression, hypertension, T2DM,
PTSD), yoga MAY add value beyond exercise through YOGA-SPECIFIC mechanisms
(pranayama → vagal tone, meditation → GABA, body awareness → interoception,
transcriptional anti-inflammatory). But this remains a 🟡 synthesis —
large-scale dismantling studies separating yoga-specific from exercise-
equivalent contributions have not been done.

## §8 — INDIVIDUAL VARIATION & CONTRAINDICATIONS

### §8.1 — Safety Profile 🟢

**Cramer et al. 2015** (Am J Epidemiol): 301 RCTs searched, 94 reporting
adverse events (n=8,430 total). KEY FINDING: No difference in frequency of
intervention-related, nonserious, or serious adverse events, nor in adverse
event-related dropouts, comparing yoga vs usual care OR vs other exercise.
Yoga is about as safe as exercise generally. 🟢

**Cramer et al. 2013** (PLOS ONE): 35 case reports + 2 case series = 76 total
adverse event cases. Most: musculoskeletal (sprains/strains), self-limited. 🟢

**Overall:** Yoga's serious-adverse-event rate is LOW and statistically
indistinguishable from other exercise or usual care. Most reported harms are
musculoskeletal and self-limited. This places B16 among the SAFEST therapies
in Track B, alongside B15 (Wayne 2014: safest) and B03 (massage, very low
adverse event rate).

### §8.2 — Hot/Bikram Yoga Safety Considerations 🟡

Hot yoga (40°C/104°F) carries DISTINCT heat-related risks not shared by
room-temperature practice:
- Elevated core temperature: "considerable proportion" exceeded 103°F,
  one case >104°F (heat illness threshold)
- Case reports: hyponatremia from overhydration (serum Na 120 mmol/L),
  rhabdomyolysis, sudden cardiac arrest (rare)
- Hunter 2018 (Exp Physiol, "Bikram Yoga Heart Study"): heated vs
  thermoneutral yoga → BOTH improved endothelium-dependent vasodilation.
  Heat is NOT necessary for the vascular benefit — yoga alone drives the effect.

**Assessment:** Heat adds physiological stress and documented risk without
demonstrated additive therapeutic benefit. Parallel to B08 moxibustion:
thermal component is REAL but the question is whether it adds value BEYOND
the base therapy. For B08, heat adds unique TRPV1 pathway. For Bikram,
evidence suggests heat does NOT add therapeutic benefit (Hunter 2018). 🟡

### §8.4 — Contraindications

**Absolute contraindications:** 🟢
- Uncontrolled hypertension + inversions (baroreceptor risk)
- Acute disc herniation + extreme flexion/extension
- Unstable joints + advanced flexibility poses
- Retinal detachment risk + inversions
- Late-stage pregnancy + certain supine/twisted poses

**Relative contraindications:** 🟡
- Osteoporosis: avoid deep forward folds (vertebral compression fracture risk)
- Glaucoma: avoid inversions (intraocular pressure)
- Hot yoga: cardiovascular conditions, pregnancy, dehydration risk
- Hypermobility: Yin yoga may be CONTRAINDICATED (excessive passive stretch
  on already hypermobile joints)

## §9 — EVIDENCE ASSESSMENT

### §9.1 — Strengths

1. **Volume**: 312+ RCTs, 51+ SRs (Wang 2025 umbrella), 28,000+ participants.
   Among the LARGEST evidence bases in Track B (competing with B01 acupuncture
   and B15 Tai Chi). 🟢

2. **Top-tier publications**: Cochrane (2022), Ann Intern Med (Saper 2017,
   Tilbrook 2011), JAMA Psychiatry (Simon 2021), J Clin Psychiatry (van der
   Kolk 2014). 🟢

3. **Active comparators**: Unlike many Track B therapies, yoga has been
   tested against ACTIVE controls (stretching, PT, CBT, walking, exercise).
   This strengthens negative findings (yoga ≈ exercise) as much as positive
   ones. 🟢

4. **Diverse populations**: Low-income/racially diverse (Saper 2017), elderly,
   cancer survivors, veterans with PTSD, women specifically. 🟢

5. **Safety data**: Large-scale safety review (Cramer 2015, n=8,430). 🟢

6. **Guideline adoption**: ACP, NICE, VA/DoD include yoga for LBP. 🟢

### §9.2 — Weaknesses

1. **Style heterogeneity**: "Yoga" is not ONE intervention. Hatha ≠ Yin ≠
   Ashtanga ≠ Restorative. Most RCTs use "Hatha" or don't specify style.
   Meta-analyses pool diverse styles as if interchangeable. This is analogous
   to meta-analyzing "exercise" without distinguishing swimming from
   weightlifting — meaningful heterogeneity is obscured. 🟡

2. **Component confounding**: No clean dismantling study separates posture
   vs breathing vs meditation contributions. When yoga "works," which
   component(s) drove the effect? This is the SINGLE BIGGEST methodological
   gap in yoga research. 🟡

3. **Blinding impossible**: Same as B15. Participants know they are doing yoga.
   Expectations, placebo, and attention effects cannot be controlled. 🟢

4. **Yoga ≈ exercise for pain**: The most consistent finding (Sherman 2011,
   Saper 2017, Cochrane 2022) challenges yoga-specific mechanism claims for
   MSK conditions. If stretching works equally well, the yoga "brand" may
   contribute more through scaffolding (self-efficacy, philosophical framework,
   social context) than through unique physiology. 🟡

5. **Research skewed to gentle styles**: Mechanistic studies concentrated in
   Hatha/Iyengar/Restorative. Vigorous styles (Vinyasa, Ashtanga, Power) are
   under-studied at the mechanistic level despite popularity. Generalizing
   across the spectrum is unjustified. 🟡

6. **GABA hypothesis still unconfirmed**: Streeter's work is influential but
   based on small samples (n < 30 per group) and is explicitly a "Medical
   Hypotheses" paper. Needs large-scale replication. 🟡

7. **Cultural/geographic diversity**: Unlike B15 (geographic bias toward
   China), yoga research is more internationally distributed. But an
   opposite concern applies: many RCTs are from INDIA, where cultural
   familiarity and expectations may amplify effects beyond what would be
   observed in yoga-naive Western populations. 🟡

## §10 — OPEN QUESTIONS

**Q1: Is yoga's efficacy from yoga-specific or exercise-equivalent mechanisms?** 🟡 CRITICAL
The consistent yoga ≈ exercise finding for MSK pain suggests exercise-equivalent
mechanisms dominate for PAIN. But Streeter 2010 (yoga > walking for mood/GABA)
suggests yoga-specific mechanisms MAY contribute for DEPRESSION/AUTONOMIC conditions.
A large-scale dismantling RCT (yoga components vs matched exercise vs each component
alone) is the definitive test. Without it, the field cannot distinguish yoga-specific
from exercise-equivalent contributions.

**Q2: Does the GABA hypothesis survive replication?** 🟡
Streeter's work is influential but based on small samples. Does yoga → ↑GABA
replicate in a large (n > 100) multicenter RCT? Is the GABA increase causal for
mood improvement, or a correlate? Is the effect specific to yoga or shared with
other mind-body practices (tai chi, meditation)?

**Q3: Which yoga component drives which outcome?** 🟡
Asana, pranayama, and meditation have NEVER been systematically separated in an
RCT. Does pranayama alone produce the depression benefit? Does asana alone
produce the LBP benefit? Can components be "prescribed" independently?

**Q4: How do different styles compare head-to-head?** 🟡
Almost no head-to-head RCTs of Yin vs Hatha vs Vinyasa vs Iyengar for the SAME
condition. The style-dependent mechanism profiles (§5.3, §6.2) are largely
THEORETICAL, derived from biomechanical reasoning rather than clinical comparison.

**Q5: Does Yin yoga produce measurable fascial remodeling?** 🟡
The theoretical rationale (sustained load → viscoelastic creep → fascial
remodeling) is sound (A03 §1.2), but empirical verification is limited. Does
long-term Yin practice show measurable changes in fascial thickness, hydration,
or stiffness by ultrasound/MRI? Same structural verification gap flagged for
deep tissue massage (A03 §8 Q8, Gao 2026).

**Q6: What is yoga's optimal dose-response?** 🟡
Dose-response data exists for cancer fatigue (~220 min/week optimal) and cortisol
(~300 MET-weeks). But dose-response curves for depression, BP, T2DM, and pain
are not established. Frequency × duration × intensity × style interactions are
unknown.

**Q7: Does yoga produce lasting autonomic retuning?** 🟡
A02 §8 Q3 flags this: yoga practitioners have higher resting vagal tone, but
selection bias is a concern (people with higher vagal tone may choose yoga).
RCT evidence for LASTING (beyond treatment period) autonomic retuning is limited.
If real, what is the minimum maintenance dose?

**Q8: Does yoga-specific interoceptive training transfer to daily life?** 🟡
Yoga improves interoceptive accuracy in a single session (heartbeat counting).
Does this transfer to improved body-signal detection in daily life? Does it
improve healthcare decision-making or symptom recognition? Connects to
Human-Predictive-Drive Interoception (Keystone #17).
