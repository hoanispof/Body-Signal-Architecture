# B01 — Acupuncture

> **Scope:** The insertion of thin metallic needles into specific body points to produce
> therapeutic effects. Includes manual acupuncture, electroacupuncture (EA), laser
> acupuncture, and related microsystem techniques (auricular, scalp, hand).
>
> **Evidence scale:** The largest evidence base of any body-based therapy — 862 systematic
> reviews covering 184 conditions (Birch 2025), 56 Cochrane reviews, ATC meta-analysis
> of 39 trials/20,827 patients (Vickers 2012/2018). TIER 1 multi-session treatment.
>
> **Framing:** Traditional theoretical constructs (Qi, meridians, Yin-Yang, Five Elements)
> are recorded here AS DATA — what practitioners believe and use for clinical decision-
> making — NOT endorsed as mechanism. Mechanisms are addressed separately in §5 using
> modern neuroscience, cross-referenced with Track A.
>
> **Track A cross-reference:** All 9 Track A systems are activated by acupuncture.
> This file adds ACUPUNCTURE-SPECIFIC detail not covered in Track A.

## §1 — DEFINITION & ORIGIN

### §1.1 — What Acupuncture Is

```
🟢 PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION:

  Acupuncture is a therapeutic intervention involving the insertion of thin,
  solid metallic needles (typically 0.12–0.40mm diameter, stainless steel)
  through the skin into underlying tissue at specific anatomically-defined
  points (acupoints). Needles are manipulated manually (rotation, lifting-
  thrusting) or stimulated electrically to produce local and systemic effects.

  THE CORE EMPIRICAL OBSERVATION:
    Stimulating specific body points produces reproducible therapeutic effects
    on conditions both local (musculoskeletal pain at the site) and distant
    (nausea via wrist point P6, gastric motility via leg point ST36).

  This observation is documented across:
    → 2,000+ years of clinical practice in East Asia
    → 862 systematic reviews covering 184 conditions (Birch 2025)
    → 2,189 guideline recommendations across 204 health problems (27 years)
    → Animal models where placebo is not possible (Goldman 2010)

  FORMS COVERED IN THIS FILE:
    ① Manual (hand) acupuncture — classical technique, needle manipulation
    ② Electroacupuncture (EA) — electrical stimulation through inserted needles
    ③ Laser acupuncture — non-invasive photobiomodulation at acupoints
    ④ Microsystems — auricular (ear), scalp, hand subsystems
    ⑤ Related techniques — intradermal needles, press tacks, moxibustion-on-needle

  🟢 Physical description: verifiable
  🟢 Clinical observation: supported by meta-analyses
  🔴 Traditional explanatory framework (Qi/meridians): recorded as data (§1.3)
```

```
  │ 11  │ Gallbladder   │ GB   │ Foot Shaoyang  │ LR    │ 23:00–01:00  │
  │ 12  │ Liver         │ LR   │ Foot Jueyin    │ GB    │ 01:00–03:00  │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  8 EXTRAORDINARY VESSELS (Qi Jing Ba Mai 奇经八脉):
    Du Mai (Governing) — posterior midline, "sea of Yang meridians"
    Ren Mai (Conception) — anterior midline, "sea of Yin meridians"
    Chong Mai (Penetrating) — "Sea of Blood," tied to Yuan Qi
    Dai Mai (Belt/Girdle) — only horizontal vessel, binds longitudinal channels
    Yin Qiao Mai / Yang Qiao Mai (Heel Vessels) — regulate leg/sleep-wake
    Yin Wei Mai / Yang Wei Mai (Linking Vessels) — link Yin or Yang channels
    → Only Ren and Du have their own acupoints; others borrow from regular channels.
    → First systematized by Li Shizhen, Qijing Bamai Kao (~1578).

  ADDITIONAL CHANNEL CATEGORIES:
    15 Luo-Connecting Vessels (Luo Mai) — branch from regular channels
    12 Divergent Channels (Jing Bie) — deep branches connecting to organs
    12 Sinew Channels (Jing Jin) — musculoskeletal distributions, no own points
    12 Cutaneous Regions (Pi Bu) — skin zones per meridian

  TOTAL: 361 classical acupoints on the 14 main channels
    (12 principal + Ren Mai + Du Mai)

  MODERN EVALUATION (→ A09 for full analysis):
    → Meridians as anatomical channels carrying Qi: NOT FOUND (A09 §1)
    → Clinical observations (acupoint-organ effects): CONFIRMED measurable
    → Best current explanation: multi-system convergence zones (A09 §4)
    → 93% trigger point-acupoint overlap (Dorsher 2008, → A08 §4)
    → 80% acupoints at fascial planes (Langevin 2002, → A03 §3.2)
    → PROKR2 molecular basis for specificity (Liu 2021, → A02 §3.5)

  🟢 System structure: documented since Neijing, WHO-standardized
  🔴 Qi flow through channels: traditional claim, not anatomically verified
  🟢 Clinical effects at specific points: confirmed by multiple meta-analyses
```

#### §1.3.3 — Yin-Yang (阴阳)

```
DATA — FOUNDATIONAL ORGANIZING PRINCIPLE:

  Two complementary, interdependent, mutually-opposing qualities whose
  dynamic balance = health, imbalance = disease.

  CLINICAL APPLICATION IN ACUPUNCTURE:
    Yang grouping: Exterior / Heat / Excess
    Yin grouping: Interior / Cold / Deficiency

  Four imbalance patterns: Yin deficiency, Yang deficiency,
  Yin excess, Yang excess → determine treatment strategy.

  Needling aims to: tonify deficiency, drain excess, warm cold,
  clear heat — restoring Yin-Yang equilibrium.
```

#### §1.3.5 — Zang-Fu (脏腑) Organ Theory

```
DATA — TCM ORGAN SYSTEM:

  5 ZANG (脏, Yin/solid, storing):
    Heart — governs Blood, houses Shen (spirit/consciousness)
    Liver — governs free flow of Qi, stores Blood, controls tendons
    Spleen — governs transformation/transportation, holds Blood in vessels
    Lung — governs Qi and respiration, controls skin/body hair
    Kidney — stores Essence (Jing), governs water, controls bones/marrow

  6 FU (腑, Yang/hollow, transforming):
    Small Intestine — separates pure from turbid
    Large Intestine — controls passage and conduction
    Stomach — receives and decomposes food ("sea of grain and water")
    Gallbladder — stores/excretes bile, governs decision-making
    Bladder — stores and excretes urine
    San Jiao (Triple Burner) — governs water/Qi passages (Upper/Middle/Lower)
      No single discrete anatomical structure.

  PERICARDIUM (心包): Added as 6th Zang to match 12 meridians with 6 Zang-Fu
  pairs. Functions as Heart's protective envelope. Paired with San Jiao.

  NOTE: TCM "organs" are FUNCTIONAL SYSTEMS, not identical to anatomical
  organs. TCM "Spleen" includes aspects of pancreatic and digestive function.
  TCM "Kidney" encompasses adrenal function and reproductive endocrinology.
```

#### §1.3.6 — Pattern Differentiation (辨证 Bian Zheng)

```
DATA — 6 DIAGNOSTIC FRAMEWORKS:

  Pattern differentiation = TCM's diagnostic methodology for determining
  treatment strategy. Each framework offers a different analytical lens:

  ① EIGHT PRINCIPLES (八纲 Ba Gang):
     The overarching framework. Four paired categories:
       Yin / Yang (overall classification)
       Exterior / Interior (disease location)
       Cold / Heat (disease nature)
       Deficiency / Excess (body resistance vs pathogen strength)
     Applied first, before more specific differentiation.

  ② ZANG-FU PATTERNS:
     Most clinically granular system. Identifies which organ(s) are
     dysfunctional and how. Examples: Liver Qi Stagnation, Kidney Yang
     Deficiency, Spleen Qi Sinking. Combines Eight Principles + organ
     localization + Qi-Blood-Fluid analysis.

  ③ SIX STAGES (六经辨证, Zhang Zhongjing, Shang Han Lun, ~200 CE):
     Models sequential inward disease progression for cold-damage:
       Taiyang → Yangming → Shaoyang (3 Yang, exterior→middle)
       Taiyin → Shaoyin → Jueyin (3 Yin, deeper interior)

  ④ FOUR LEVELS (卫气营血, Ye Tianshi, Wen Re Lun, ~1746):
     For warm/febrile disease (distinct from cold-damage paradigm):
       Wei (Defensive) → Qi → Ying (Nutritive) → Xue (Blood)
     Surface → deep progression.

  ⑤ SAN JIAO DIFFERENTIATION (Wu Jutong, Wen Bing Tiao Bian, 1798/1813):
     Vertical/anatomical progression for warm disease:
       Upper Jiao (Lung, Pericardium/Heart)
       Middle Jiao (Spleen, Stomach)
       Lower Jiao (Liver, Kidney)

  ⑥ QI-BLOOD-BODY FLUID PATTERNS:
     Diagnoses via fundamental substances:
       Qi patterns: Deficiency, Stagnation, Sinking, Counterflow
       Blood (Xue): Deficiency, Stasis, Heat, Cold
       Body Fluid (Jinye): Deficiency/Dryness, Phlegm/Damp accumulation
     Core principle: "Qi commands Blood, Blood is mother of Qi."
     Qi stagnation pain = moving/distending; Blood stasis pain = fixed/stabbing.

  CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE: Treatment plans (point selection, needle technique,
  herbs) are determined by the identified PATTERN, not the Western disease
  diagnosis. Same Western disease → different TCM patterns → different
  acupoint prescriptions. This is why acupuncture prescriptions vary
  between patients with the same Western diagnosis.

  🟢 Diagnostic frameworks: well-documented clinical systems
  🟡 Diagnostic validity: some patterns correlate with measurable states
       (e.g., "Blood Stasis" ↔ hypercoagulability), others do not
```

### §1.4 — Standardization

```
🟢 WHO STANDARDIZATION:

  361 STANDARD ACUPOINTS:
    → 309 on the 12 principal meridians
    → 52 on Ren Mai and Du Mai
    → WHO-WPRO Standard Nomenclature Part 1 (1984), adopted 1991
    → Locations refined: WHO Standard Point Locations (2008)
      92 initially disputed → 86 resolved through 11 consultations

  48 EXTRA POINTS (经外奇穴):
    → WHO-WPRO Part 2 (1988)
    → Not on 14 main channels but recognized for clinical effectiveness
    → Clear anatomical location, ≥0.5cm from any classical point
    → Examples: Yintang (EX-HN3, "third eye"), Taiyang (EX-HN5, temple)

  ASHI POINTS (阿是穴):
    → Not fixed/standardized — identified by palpation (tender/reactive)

    → Named for patient's exclamation: "Ah, yes!" (阿是)
    → Frequently compared to myofascial trigger points (→ A08 §4)

    Three parallel designations per point:
      ① Alphanumeric code: meridian abbreviation + number (e.g., LI4, ST36)
      ② Hanyu Pinyin romanization (e.g., Hegu, Zusanli)
      ③ Original Han characters (e.g., 合谷, 足三里)

  HISTORICAL POINT COUNT EVOLUTION:
    349 (Zhenjiu Jiayi Jing, 3rd c.) → 354 (Wang Weiyi, 1027)
    → 361 (modern WHO standard)
```

```
      appointed guild head ~1683; 1694 = death year, not invention year)
    → Legend: discovered the principle after pricking himself on a pine
      needle inside bamboo wrapping during a retreat at Enoshima Cave
    → Mechanism: thin hollow tube (plastic/metal) holds needle upright;
      tapped to seat tip past epidermis, then withdrawn before advancing

      Shonishin techniques.
```

### §2.2 — Insertion and Manipulation Techniques

```
🟢 INSERTION PARAMETERS:

  ANGLE:
    Perpendicular (90°) — most body acupoints
    Oblique (~45°) — near vital organs/vessels, thin tissue areas
    Transverse (~15°) — scalp, face, areas with thin muscle layer

  DEPTH (point-specific, adjusted for patient build/age/region):
    Superficial: 2-5mm (face, scalp, ears, elderly/infants)
    Standard: 10-25mm (most body points)
    Deep: 25-50mm+ (gluteal, lumbar, abdominal, muscular adults)
    → Traditionally measured in cun (proportional body-inch, → §3.4)
    → Example: ST36 (Zusanli) oblique — mean effective depth ~22mm

  LIFTING-THRUSTING (提插 Ti-Cha):
    Vertical needle movement within the tissue after insertion.
    Standard amplitude: 3-5mm (light), 5-10mm (moderate), >10mm (strong)
    Standard frequency: ~60 cycles/min (light), up to 120+/min (strong)
    (Lyu et al. 2019, Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine)

  TWIRLING/ROTATING (捻转 Nian-Zhuan):
    Bidirectional rotation of needle along its axis.
    Light: ~180° amplitude, slow
    Strong: ≥360° amplitude, rapid
    CRITICAL: beyond 360°, collagen fibers begin to WIND around the shaft
    → creates "needle grasp" (→ §2.3, → A03 §3.2)
    → biomechanically distinct from sub-360° rotation
    → 4 rev/s superior to 2 rev/s for analgesia (PMC8526210)

🟡 TONIFICATION (补 Bu) vs SEDATION (泻 Xie):

  Traditional manipulation rules for treating deficiency vs excess patterns:

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Feature          │ Bu (Tonify)          │ Xie (Sedate)             │
  │───────────────────┼──────────────────────┼──────────────────────────│
  │  Insertion speed  │ Slow, gentle         │ Rapid                    │
  │  Thrust vs lift   │ Heavy thrust in,     │ Gentle thrust in,        │
  │                   │ gentle lift out      │ vigorous lift out        │
  │  Rotation         │ Small amplitude,     │ Large amplitude,         │
  │                   │ low frequency        │ higher frequency         │
  │  Needle withdrawal│ Slow, hole pressed   │ Swift, hole left open    │
  │  Clinical use     │ Xu (deficiency)      │ Shi (excess) patterns    │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  CAVEAT: Bu/Xie technique descriptions vary meaningfully between classical
  sources (Nan Jing vs Ming compilations). The table reflects the most
  commonly cited modern textbook synthesis, not a single authoritative source.

  NEEDLE RETENTION TIME:
    Typical: 15-30 minutes (median 30 min in RCTs)
    Range: 15-60 minutes depending on style and condition
    Evidence: treatment effectiveness may not depend on retention duration
    once a minimum threshold is reached (Oh et al. 2018, Integrative
    Cancer Therapies)

  NEEDLES PER SESSION:
    Typical: 5-20 needles (commonly 8-12 local + 4-6 distal)
    Complex protocols: up to 18-30 points

  🟢 Insertion parameters: published/measured (Lyu 2019)
  🟡 Bu/Xie rules: documented tradition, cross-source variation noted
```

### §2.3 — De Qi (得气, Arrival of Qi)

```
🟢 DE QI — THE COMPOSITE THERAPEUTIC SENSATION:

  PATIENT SENSATION (classical four descriptors):
    酸 suan — aching/soreness
    麻 ma — numbness/tingling
    胀 zhang — distension/fullness/pressure
    重 zhong — heaviness
    Also: warmth, spreading/radiating, "electric" quality, dull ache, pulling

  QUANTITATIVE ASSESSMENT:
    MASS Index (Massachusetts General Hospital Acupuncture Sensation Scale):
      12 visual-analog-scale descriptors: soreness, aching, deep pressure,
      heaviness, fullness/distention, tingling, numbness, sharp pain,
      dull pain, warmth, cold, throbbing
      (Jang et al. 2017, PMC5318627)
    Sensation propagation occurs in 78% of trials (Zhou/Fang et al. 2011)
    Sharp pain in only 4.6% of trials (= technical error, not De Qi)

  PRACTITIONER SENSATION — "NEEDLE GRASP":
    Classical metaphor (Zhenjiu Dacheng): "like a fish biting a fishing line"
    Felt as increased tissue resistance, tugging grip on needle
    Tissue shifts from loose ("inserting into sand") to firm ("held by magnet")

  NEUROPHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS:
    → Composite activation of A-beta (touch/pressure), A-delta (fast pain),
      and C-fiber (slow/polymodal) afferents (→ A01 §1.2)
    → De Qi is qualitatively DISTINCT from sharp pain:
      Sharp pain = primarily A-delta nociceptive (localized, fast)
      De Qi = multi-fiber composite (diffuse, heavy, spreading)
    → fMRI evidence: De Qi and sharp pain show partly separable brain
      activation patterns (Sun et al. 2013, PMC3582103; PMC3544542)
    → De Qi associated with somatosensory/pain processing cortical
      activation AND limbic deactivation

  CONNECTIVE TISSUE MECHANISM (→ A03 §3.2):
    Langevin's needle-grasp chain:
      Needle rotation → collagen fibers wind around shaft → "whorl" of
      wound tissue → sustained mechanical stretch → fibroblast
      cytoskeletal remodeling (Rho/Rac pathways) → ATP release →
      adenosine → A1 receptor → local analgesia (Goldman 2010: 24-fold
      adenosine increase, absent in A1-KO mice)
    Peak fibroblast response: 2 revolutions (dose-dependent)
    Signal radius: several centimeters from needle
    Whorl persists after needle removal → prolonged effect

  IS DE QI NECESSARY FOR THERAPEUTIC EFFECT?
    TCM position: essential — diagnostic sign and prognostic indicator
    Modern evidence: INCONCLUSIVE (Zhang 2013, EBCAM 750140 — systematic
    review found conflicting results across De Qi-present vs -absent trials)
    → Several Japanese minimal-stimulation protocols achieve clinical effects
      WITHOUT deliberately eliciting strong De Qi
    → Some autonomic evidence: De Qi correlates with parasympathetic
      upregulation (HRV changes)
    → STRICTA (2010) requires De Qi reporting in clinical trials

  🟢 Sensation descriptors: validated scales (MASS, ASQ)
  🟢 Connective tissue mechanism: Langevin 2001/2002/2006/2007
  🟡 Neurophysiological basis: supported but not fully mapped
  🟡 Necessity debate: genuinely unresolved
```

### §2.5 — Laser Acupuncture

```
🟢 LASER ACUPUNCTURE — NON-INVASIVE PHOTOBIOMODULATION AT ACUPOINTS:

  WAVELENGTHS:
    632.8nm (red, HeNe) — original clinical wavelength, shallow penetration
    780-860nm (infrared, GaAlAs) — primary modern range, deeper penetration
    405nm (violet) — newer, very shallow (~2mm), antibacterial effects

  POWER AND DOSE:
    Power output: 1-500 mW
    Energy density: variable, no single standard — commonly cited ~4 J/cm²
    WALT guidelines: 1-4J (904nm), 4-16J (780-860nm) per point
    Key problem: dose depends on wavelength, power, tissue type, condition
    → No agreed international dosing standard (Litscher 2012 attempted)

  EVIDENCE:
    49 RCTs pooled: favorable for musculoskeletal pain vs placebo
    (Law et al. 2015, PubMed 25660439)
    Knee OA: short-term pain improvement, but effect disappears by 8 weeks
    Safety: generally safe, few mild/transient adverse events (PMC7455477)

  ADVANTAGES vs NEEDLE:
    ✓ Painless, non-invasive, no infection risk
    ✓ Well-tolerated in needle-phobic patients and children
    ✓ Electronically standardizable dose
  LIMITATIONS:
    ✗ Shallow penetration limits use to superficial points
    ✗ No mechanical De Qi/needle-grasp component (different mechanism)
    ✗ Effect sizes may be weaker/shorter-lived than needle acupuncture
    ✗ Inconsistent dosing across studies

  🟢 Mechanism: photobiomodulation, distinct from mechanical needling
  🟡 Evidence: positive but weaker and less consistent than manual/EA
  🟡 Standardization: dosing guidelines not yet established
```

### §2.6 — Treatment Dose Parameters

```
🟢 STANDARD TREATMENT PROTOCOLS:

  SESSION DURATION: 20-30 minutes (range 15-60 min)
    → Median in RCTs: 30 minutes
    → Retention time may not affect outcome once minimum threshold reached
      (Oh et al. 2018)

  TREATMENT FREQUENCY:
    Acute conditions: 2-3 sessions/week (first 2-3 weeks)
    Chronic conditions: 1-2 sessions/week, tapering
    Staged model: "relief care" (4-8 weeks, frequent)
      → "corrective care" (4-12 weeks, reduced)
      → "maintenance care" (every 2-4 weeks or monthly)

  COURSE OF TREATMENT:
    Assessment period: 3-6 sessions (to evaluate response)
    Chronic pain: commonly 6-12 sessions over several weeks
    No universal standard — condition- and response-dependent

  DOSE-RESPONSE EVIDENCE (Vickers ATC, 17,922 patients):
    vs no-acupuncture: clear dose-response
      → Effect size increased ~0.10 per additional 5 sessions
      → More needles + more sessions = better outcomes
    vs sham acupuncture: dose-response largely DISAPPEARS
      → Style, needle count, session number, practitioner experience
        showed little effect-size modification
    IMPLICATION: dose-response exists relative to doing nothing,
    but is much weaker relative to sham — bearing on mechanism debate
    (MacPherson et al. 2013, PLOS ONE, PMC3795671)

  🟢 Treatment parameters: documented clinical practice
  🟢 Dose-response: Vickers ATC IPD meta-analysis
```

### §3.2 — Point Selection Logic

```
🟡 CLINICAL POINT SELECTION — INTEGRATING MULTIPLE STRATEGIES:

  TCM practitioners combine multiple selection strategies per treatment:

  ① LOCAL POINTS: Near the affected area
     → Shoulder pain → local shoulder points (LI15, TE14, SI9)
     → Basis: direct stimulation of local nerves/tissue (→ A01 §3.4 axon reflex)

  ② DISTAL POINTS: Far from affected area, on same channel
     → Shoulder pain (LI channel) → LI4 (hand), LI11 (elbow)
     → Basis: somatovisceral reflex, segmental convergence (→ A07 §1)
     → Classical principle: "distal points treat upper, upper points treat lower"

  ③ ADJACENT POINTS: Near but not at the affected area
     → Eye problems → Yintang (between eyebrows), BL2 (above eye)

  ④ SYMPTOMATIC/EMPIRICAL POINTS: Historically effective for specific symptoms
     → LI4 (Hegu) for facial pain/headache — empirical, supported by
       trigeminal convergence at spinal nucleus (→ A07)
     → ST36 (Zusanli) for gastrointestinal disorders — validated by
       PROKR2-vagal pathway (Liu 2021 Nature, → A02 §3.5)
     → PC6 (Neiguan) for nausea — validated by 26 Cochrane trials

  ⑤ ZANG-FU PATTERN-BASED: Based on TCM diagnosis
     → Liver Qi Stagnation → LR3, LR14, PC6, GB34
     → Kidney Yang Deficiency → BL23, GV4, KI3, KI7 + moxa

  ⑥ MOTHER-CHILD (Five Element logic):
     → Tonify deficiency via the "mother" element point (→ §3.1 Five Shu)

  ASHI POINTS (阿是穴):
    Not a formal category but a selection METHOD:
    → Identified by palpation — tender/reactive points
    → No fixed location — patient-specific
    → Principle: "take the pain as the point" (以痛为腧, Lingshu Ch.13)
    → Named by Sun Simiao (孫思邈, 652 CE, Beiji Qianjin Yaofang):
      "Ah, yes!" (阿是) = patient's exclamation when tender spot found
    → Underlying concept predates the name (Lingshu sinew channels chapter)
    → Comparable to trigger point therapy (→ A08 §4)
    → 70% trigger point-acupoint overlap (Melzack 1977), 93% (Dorsher 2008)

  TYPICAL PRESCRIPTION STRUCTURE:
    Host points (主穴): core points for the primary pattern/condition
    + Adjunct points (配穴): additional points for secondary symptoms
    + Local points: for specific affected area
    = 8-20 needles total

  🟡 Selection logic: traditional clinical art + emerging neuroanatomical support
  🟢 Specific point validations: ST36-vagal (Liu 2021), PC6-PONV (Cochrane)
```

### §3.3 — Microsystems

```
🟢 MICROSYSTEM THEORY: specific body regions contain a complete map of
  the whole body in miniature. Stimulating points within the microsystem
  treats corresponding body regions/organs.

  NOTE: Microsystem theory is covered here as POINT SYSTEMS (where/what).
  Detailed tradition descriptions and variant comparisons → §7.
```

#### Auricular (Ear) Acupuncture

```
🟢 THE MOST DEVELOPED MICROSYSTEM:

  THEORETICAL BASIS:
    Paul Nogier (Lyon, 1956/1957): proposed the "inverted fetus" model —
    the external ear maps the body as an inverted fetus:
      Lobule = head/face
      Antihelix = spine
      Concha = internal organs
      Tragus = nose/throat
    Chinese ear acupuncture developed in parallel, with some differences
    in point locations.

  STANDARDIZED POINTS:
    WHO standardized auricular nomenclature
    Dense vagal innervation of concha (auricular branch of vagus nerve,
    ABVN) → provides direct parasympathetic pathway (→ A02 §3.4)

  NADA 5-POINT PROTOCOL (National Acupuncture Detoxification Association):
    For addiction, PTSD, anxiety, disaster relief:
      ① Shen Men (Spirit Gate) — calming, analgesic
      ② Sympathetic — autonomic regulation
      ③ Kidney — source Qi, willpower (TCM), adrenal (Western)
      ④ Liver — detoxification, anger regulation
      ⑤ Lung — grief, immune function, respiration
    Mixed RCT evidence despite wide adoption in >1,000 clinics worldwide

  BATTLEFIELD ACUPUNCTURE (BFA):
    Developed by Col. Richard Niemtzow, MD, PhD (formulated Aug 2002,
    first published: Medical Acupuncture 2007;19(4):225-228)
    5 ASP gold semi-permanent needles in specific sequence:
      ① Cingulate Gyrus → ② Thalamus → ③ Omega 2 →
      ④ Shen Men → ⑤ Point Zero
    Adopted by US VA/DoD: 2,800+ providers trained
    Cost: $4.05/session vs $115-294/week opioids
    Reported: 75-82% immediate pain reduction
    → ASP needles retained 3-4 days until natural expulsion

  ADVANCED CONCEPTS (Nogier school):
    VAS/RAC (Vascular Autonomic Signal / Réflexe Auriculo-Cardiaque):
      Pulse-based diagnostic method — practitioner palpates radial pulse
      while probing ear points; change in pulse quality = active point
    3 Phases of ear point activity:
      Phase 1: active disease (standard locations)
      Phase 2: chronic/degenerative (inverted/shifted locations)
      Phase 3: cellular/energetic (further shifted)
    Nogier frequencies: A through G (specific Hz assignments), used with
      laser/electrical stimulation

  🟢 ABVN vagal innervation: anatomically verified (→ A02)
  🟡 Inverted fetus model: empirically useful, anatomical basis partial
  🟡 NADA evidence: widely adopted but RCT evidence mixed
  🟢 BFA: VA-adopted, cost-effectiveness documented
  🔴 Phases 2-3, Nogier frequencies: limited independent validation
```

#### Scalp Acupuncture

```
🟢 CORTICAL MAPPING ON THE SCALP:

  Three major systems, each mapping cerebral cortex regions onto the scalp:

  ① JIAO SHUNFA SYSTEM (焦顺发, 1970s, China):
     Cortical mapping approach — treatment zones on scalp correspond to
     underlying cortical functional areas:
       Motor Area (MS 6): contralateral motor cortex → paralysis
       Sensory Area (MS 7): contralateral sensory cortex → numbness/pain
       Foot Motor-Sensory Area (MS 8): midline vertex → lower limb
       Speech Areas (MS 1-3): Broca/Wernicke zones → aphasia
       Tremor/Chorea Area (MS 9): → movement disorders
     14 standard scalp lines defined by WHO-WPRO (1988)
     Most evidence for: stroke rehabilitation, movement disorders

  ② YNSA (Yamamoto New Scalp Acupuncture, 山元式新頭鍼療法):
     Toshikatsu Yamamoto (Japan, 1960s-present)
     Multiple somatotopes (body maps) on scalp — A-I points on forehead/
     temporal region (each = body area)
     Unique: DIAGNOSIS through neck and abdominal palpation
     → Palpate neck/abdomen to identify reactive zones
     → Corresponding scalp point is needled
     → Monitor palpation change as treatment feedback

  ③ ZHU'S SCALP ACUPUNCTURE (朱氏头皮针):
     Zhu Mingqing (USA/China)
     Dynamic technique: patient performs ACTIVE MOVEMENT while needles
     are stimulated in scalp zones → enhanced cortical activation +
     neuroplastic effect
     Used primarily for pain and neurological conditions

  🟢 Jiao system: WHO-standardized scalp lines
  🟡 YNSA: practitioner-developed, growing evidence base
  🟡 Zhu's: smaller evidence base, interesting mechanism hypothesis
```

#### Hand Microsystem

```
🟡 KOREAN HAND THERAPY (KHT, 高麗手指鍼):

  Developed by Tae Woo Yoo (유태우, 1971, South Korea)
  345 points + 14 micro-meridians mapped on the hand (entire body):
    Middle finger = head/spine
    Ring + index fingers = arms
    Thumb + little finger = legs
    Palm = torso (front)
    Dorsum = torso (back)

  Theoretical basis: hand as complete body microsystem (resembles
  a standing human figure with arms raised)

  Treatment: fine needles, hand magnets, press pellets (Seoam pellets)
  Popular in Korea (integrated into some clinical curricula)

  🟡 KHT: clinically used in Korea, limited international RCT evidence
```

#### Other Microsystems

```
  DIEN CHAN (面诊, Vietnamese Facial Reflexology):
    Developed by Bui Quoc Chau (Bùi Quốc Châu, 1980, Ho Chi Minh City)
    Multiple overlapping face maps (reported 20+ maps)
    Non-invasive: uses blunt tools, pressure, NOT needles
    🟡 Vietnamese clinical use, minimal Western RCT evidence.
    Retired from Track B (~0 RCTs; method expanded beyond
    facial reflexology, contradicting core premise).

  ABDOMINAL ACUPUNCTURE (腹针, Bo Zhiyun 薄智云):
    "Turtle diagram" — maps body onto abdomen around umbilicus
    Shallow needling, used for chronic pain + internal medicine
    🟡 Growing evidence base, distinct mechanism hypothesis

  WRIST-ANKLE ACUPUNCTURE (腕踝针, Zhang Xinshu 张心曙):
    6 zones on each wrist and ankle (12 total)
    Shallow subcutaneous insertion, primarily for pain
    Minimal sensation during treatment (no De Qi sought)
    🟡 Chinese clinical use, limited international evidence

  🔴 MICROSYSTEM GENERAL ASSESSMENT:
    The claim that any single body region contains a complete map of the
    whole body lacks a verified anatomical mechanism. Possible explanations
    include somatotopic representation at brainstem/cortical levels, and
    vagal/autonomic reflexes (especially for auricular acupuncture where
    ABVN provides a direct neural pathway). Each microsystem requires
    independent evaluation — auricular has the strongest evidence base.
```

```
  ① PROPORTIONAL CUN (寸) MEASUREMENT:

     fixed numbers of cun regardless of individual body size:

     ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
     │  Body Segment                              │ Cun    │ Direction│
     │─────────────────────────────────────────────┼────────┼──────────│

     │  Umbilicus to pubic symphysis              │ 5      │ vertical │
     │  Axillary crease to cubital crease          │ 9      │ vertical │
     │  Cubital crease to wrist crease             │ 12     │ vertical │
     │  Greater trochanter to popliteal crease     │ 19     │ vertical │
     │  Popliteal crease to lateral malleolus      │ 16     │ vertical │
     └──────────────────────────────────────────────┴────────┴──────────┘

     Quick finger measures:
       1 cun ≈ width of thumb at IP joint
       1.5 cun ≈ width of index + middle fingers
       3 cun ≈ width of 4 fingers (index through little)

  ② ANATOMICAL LANDMARKS:
     Bony prominences, tendons, skin creases, muscle borders
     Example: LI4 (Hegu) = in the web between thumb and index finger,
     at the midpoint of the 2nd metacarpal bone on the radial side

  ③ PALPATION:
     Active point-finding by touch:
       → Tenderness/pain response (Ashi principle)
       → Texture changes (nodules, taut bands, induration)
       → Temperature differences
     Particularly emphasized in Japanese palpation-based styles
     (Kiiko Matsumoto reflex zones, YNSA diagnosis)

  ④ ELECTRICAL DETECTION:
     Point finders measuring skin electrical resistance:
       → Acupoints traditionally claimed to have lower resistance
       → Ahn 2008 systematic review: evidence INCONCLUSIVE (→ A09 §1.6)
       → Some electrical properties may reflect underlying neurovascular
         structures rather than unique "point" characteristics
       → Used clinically but scientific basis debated

  🟢 Proportional cun: WHO-standardized system
  🟢 Anatomical landmarks: verifiable anatomy
  🟡 Palpation: practitioner skill-dependent, subjective
  🟡 Electrical detection: inconclusive evidence (Ahn 2008)
```

## §4 — DOCUMENTED CLINICAL EFFECTS

```
ORGANIZATION: Evidence organized by tier using the same 3-level system
applied throughout this project:

  🟢 STRONG EVIDENCE: Multiple positive meta-analyses, guideline
     recommendations, consistent across high-quality trials
  🟡 MODERATE EVIDENCE: Positive systematic reviews, but limited by
     quality, sample size, or methodological concerns
  🔴 PRELIMINARY / EMERGING: Individual RCTs or low-quality reviews,
     insufficient for firm conclusions

NOTE: Effect sizes reported below are from the HIGHEST-QUALITY
available meta-analysis for each condition. Where both sham-controlled
and non-sham comparisons exist, sham-controlled data is prioritized
as it better isolates the specific acupuncture effect.

CAVEAT: The sham debate (§9.2) means that sham-controlled effect sizes
likely UNDERESTIMATE the true specific effect by ~39% (Luo et al. 2025).
```

### §4.1 — Strong Evidence

#### Chronic Low Back Pain

```
🟢 FIRST-LINE RECOMMENDATION IN MAJOR GUIDELINES:

  ATC 2012/2018 (Vickers et al.):
    → vs sham: SMD 0.23 (95% CI 0.13-0.33), p<.001
    → vs no-acupuncture: SMD 0.55 (95% CI 0.51-0.58), p<.001

  Cochrane (Mu et al. 2020, CD013814):
    33 trials, 8,270 participants
    → vs sham: pain MD -9.22 on 0-100 VAS (low certainty)
    → vs no treatment: pain MD -20.32 (moderate certainty)

  GERAC (Haake et al. 2007, Arch Intern Med, 167(17):1892-1898):
    1,162 patients, 340 practices:
      Verum 47.6% | Sham 44.2% | Conventional 27.4%
    → Both acupuncture groups ~2× conventional therapy
    → Verum-sham difference NOT significant (p=0.39)

  GUIDELINE RECOMMENDATIONS:
    ACP 2017 (Ann Intern Med): FIRST-LINE nonpharmacologic treatment
      for both acute/subacute AND chronic LBP (Strong recommendation)
    NICE NG193 (2021): "Consider a single course of acupuncture or
      dry needling" for chronic primary pain
    Germany: reimbursed by statutory health insurance since 2006

  🟢 Strongest evidence base in acupuncture research
```

#### Knee Osteoarthritis

```
🟢 CONDITIONALLY RECOMMENDED BY ACR/ARTHRITIS FOUNDATION:

  ATC 2012 (Vickers et al.):
    → vs sham: SMD 0.16 (95% CI 0.07-0.25), p<.001
    → vs no-acupuncture: SMD 0.57 (95% CI 0.50-0.64), p<.001

  Cochrane (Manheimer et al. 2010, CD001977):
    16 RCTs, 3,498 participants (12 knee, 3 hip, 1 mixed)
    → vs sham short-term: pain SMD -0.28 (95% CI -0.45 to -0.11)
    → vs sham 6 months: SMD -0.10 (NOT significant)
    → vs waitlist: pain SMD -0.96 (large effect)

  GERAC (Scharf et al. 2006, Ann Intern Med, 145(1):12-20):
    1,007 patients, 315 practices:
      Verum 53.1% | Sham 51.0% | Conventional 29.1%
    → Same GERAC pattern: both >> conventional, verum ≈ sham

  ACR/AF 2019 (Kolasinski et al. 2020, Arthritis Care Res, 72(2):
    149-162): CONDITIONAL recommendation for knee, hip, and/or hand OA

  🟢 vs waitlist: large effect; vs sham: small but significant short-term
  🟡 Effects diminish at 6 months vs sham
```

#### Migraine Prevention

```
🟢 NNT = 4 vs NO TREATMENT (comparable to prophylactic drugs):

  Cochrane (Linde et al. 2016, CD001218):
    22 RCTs, 4,985 participants

    vs no treatment/routine care:
      NNT = 4 (95% CI 3-6)
      → 41% acupuncture vs 17% control achieved ≥50% reduction
      At 12-month follow-up: NNT = 7 (95% CI 4-25)

    vs sham acupuncture (12 trials, 1,646 patients):
      NNT = 11 (95% CI 7-20) post-treatment
      NNT = 10 (95% CI 6-18) at follow-up
      → 50% true vs 41% sham achieved ≥50% reduction
      → SMD -0.18 (95% CI -0.28 to -0.08)

    vs prophylactic drugs (3-5 trials, 739-931 patients):
      At least non-inferior
      Headache frequency at 3 months: SMD -0.25 (favors acupuncture)
      Adverse effect rate: acupuncture 114/1000 vs drugs 341/1000
      Dropout from side effects: acupuncture 20/1000 vs drugs 71/1000

  GERAC Migraine (Diener et al. 2006, Lancet Neurol, 5(4):310-316):
    960 patients: Verum 47% | Sham 39% | Drugs 40% (NS)

  🟢 Strong evidence; comparable to drugs with fewer side effects
```

#### Tension-Type Headache

```
🟢 NNT = 3 vs ROUTINE CARE:

  Cochrane (Linde et al. 2016, CD007587):
    12 RCTs, 2,349 participants

    vs no acupuncture/routine care (2 trials):
      48% acupuncture vs 17% control (NNT = 3)
      Headache days reduced 3.9-5.8 days per month

    vs sham (7 trials, 795 participants):
      52% acupuncture vs 43% sham
      NNT = 8 (95% CI based on 11% absolute difference)
      Headache days: 1.62 fewer (95% CI 0.54-2.69)
      Intensity: minimal difference (2 units on 0-100)

  Conclusion: "Valuable nonpharmacological tool" for frequent
  episodic or chronic tension-type headache.

  🟢 Strong evidence vs routine care; small but significant vs sham
```

#### Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting (PONV)

```
🟢 PC6 STIMULATION COMPARABLE TO ANTIEMETIC DRUGS:

  Cochrane (Lee, Chan & Fan 2015, CD003281):
    59 RCTs, 7,667 participants — the largest acupuncture Cochrane review

    PC6 vs sham:
      Nausea: RR 0.68 (95% CI 0.60-0.77) — 32% reduction
        (40 trials, 4,742 participants)
      Vomiting: RR 0.60 (95% CI 0.51-0.71) — 40% reduction
        (45 trials, 5,147 participants)
      Rescue antiemetics: RR 0.64 (95% CI 0.55-0.73)
      NNT (60% baseline risk): Nausea 5, Vomiting 4

    PC6 vs antiemetic drugs:
      Nausea: RR 0.91 (NS) — NO significant difference
      Vomiting: RR 0.93 (NS) — NO significant difference
      → Authors: further comparative trials deemed "futile"

    PC6 + antiemetic vs antiemetic alone:
      Vomiting: RR 0.56 (95% CI 0.35-0.91) — additive effect

  NOTE: PC6 = Neiguan (Pericardium 6), also a Luo-Connecting point
  (→ §3.1) and Confluent point of Yin Wei Mai (→ §3.1)
  Cross-reference: → A02 §3.4 (vagal pathway)

  🟢 Largest evidence base for any single acupoint
```

#### Chemotherapy-Induced Nausea and Vomiting (CINV)

```
🟡 ELECTROACUPUNCTURE REDUCES ACUTE VOMITING:

  Cochrane (Ezzo et al. 2006, CD002285 — now WITHDRAWN):
    11 RCTs, 1,247 patients
    → EA reduced acute vomiting: RR 0.74 (95% CI 0.58-0.94, p=0.01)
    → Manual acupuncture: NOT significant for acute vomiting
    → Acupressure: reduced acute nausea (not vomiting)

  Non-Cochrane update (Yan et al. 2023):
    38 RCTs, 2,503 patients — very low certainty evidence

  Most recent (2026): 58 RCTs — improved complete control in
  overall and delayed phases; evidence quality mostly low/very low

  🟡 EA-specific benefit; manual acupuncture less clear
  🟡 Cochrane review withdrawn, no replacement
```

#### Postoperative Pain (Opioid-Sparing)

```
🟢 21-29% OPIOID REDUCTION:

  Sun et al. 2008 (Br J Anaesth, 101(2):151-160):
    15 RCTs, 1,166 patients (668 received acupuncture)

    Morphine-sparing effect:
      8 hours:  21% reduction (WMD -3.14 mg, 95% CI -5.15 to -1.14)
      24 hours: 23% reduction (WMD -8.33 mg, 95% CI -11.06 to -5.61)
      72 hours: 29% reduction (WMD -9.14 mg, 95% CI -16.07 to -2.22)

    Additional benefits:
      Nausea: RR 0.67 (reduced)
      Urinary retention: RR 0.29 (reduced)

  More recent (Wu et al. 2016, PLoS One, 11(3):e0150367):
    13 RCTs, 682 patients — opioid use SMD -0.72 (p<.05)

  CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE: In the context of the opioid crisis,
  even a 21-29% reduction in post-surgical opioid need is
  meaningful for both individual patients and public health.

  🟢 Consistent opioid-sparing effect across reviews
```

### §4.2 — Moderate Evidence

```
  evidence is limited by: small sample sizes, low methodological
  quality, heterogeneous interventions, or insufficient sham-
```

#### Chronic Neck Pain

```
🟡 DURABLE AT 3-6 MONTHS (as adjunct to active treatment):

  Fang et al. 2024 (Curr Pain Headache Rep, 28(9):957-969):
    18 RCTs — acupuncture as adjunct to active treatment:
    → 3-month pain: SMD -0.79 (95% CI -1.13 to -0.46, p<.01)
    → 6-month pain: MD -18.13 (95% CI -30.18 to -6.07, p<.01)
    → 3-month function: MD -6.06 (95% CI -8.20 to -3.92, p<.01)

  ATC 2012 (Vickers et al.):
    Neck pain vs sham: SMD 0.83 (95% CI 0.64-1.01) — LARGEST effect
    of any ATC condition (but driven by Vas trials — outlier concern)

  CAVEAT: vs SHAM specifically, NO sustained pain relief (Fang 2024:
  MD -0.12, p=0.63). Functional improvement present but not pain.

  🟡 Strong as adjunct; specific effect vs sham unclear
```

#### Fibromyalgia

```
🟡 EA SUBGROUP SIGNIFICANT; OVERALL MANUAL ACUPUNCTURE NOT:

  Overview: Choi et al. 2025 (Front Med, 12:1712065):
    13 systematic reviews mapped (2007-2022)
    AMSTAR-2: 1 high, 3 moderate, 1 low, 8 critically low

  Best sham-controlled (Kim et al. 2019, eCAM, 2019:8757685):
    10 RCTs (8 in meta-analysis, 690/561 participants):
    → Pain: SMD -0.49 (95% CI -0.79 to -0.20, p=.001) — moderate
    → Sleep: SMD -0.46 (p=.001) — high quality
    → General status: SMD -0.69 (p<.00001) — high quality
    → Fatigue: SMD -0.10 (p=.61) — NOT significant

  Cochrane (Deare et al. 2013, CD007070):
    9 RCTs, 395 participants
    → Overall vs sham: pain SMD -0.14 (NOT significant)
    → EA subgroup: pain SMD -0.63 (SIGNIFICANT)

  🟡 EA appears more effective than manual for fibromyalgia
```

#### Depression (Adjunctive)

```
🟡 STRONGEST AS ADJUNCT TO MEDICATION:

  Cochrane (Smith et al. 2018, CD004046):
    64 studies, 7,104 participants

    → vs no treatment: SMD -0.66 (5 trials, 488 pts) — low quality
    → vs sham: MD -1.69 HDRS (14 trials, 841 pts) — low quality
    → vs medication: SMD -0.23 (31 trials, 3,127 pts) — very low
    → Acupuncture + medication vs medication alone:
      SMD -1.15 (11 trials, 775 pts) — very low but "highly beneficial"

  CAVEAT: Quality rated very low across most comparisons.
  Strongest result is as ADJUNCT to antidepressants, not standalone.

  🟡 Large evidence volume but low quality throughout
```

#### Anxiety

```
🟡 SMD -0.41 — BUT COMPARISON IS VS MEDICATION, NOT SHAM:

  Yang et al. 2021 (Ann Gen Psychiatry, 20:9):
    20 RCTs, 1,823 participants (all GAD)
    → Overall: SMD -0.41 (95% CI -0.50 to -0.31, p<.001)
    → vs Western medication: SMD -0.44

  CRITICAL CAVEAT:
    → 16 of 20 studies compared acupuncture to MEDICATION
    → Only 1 of 20 used sham acupuncture as control
    → The -0.41 effect size does NOT demonstrate superiority over sham
    → 18 of 20 studies published in Chinese
    → Interpretation: acupuncture may be non-inferior to anxiolytics,
      but specific effect beyond placebo is NOT established

  🟡 Moderate effect vs medication; specific effect unclear
```

#### Insomnia

```
🟡 POSITIVE SHAM-CONTROLLED DATA:

  Yu et al. 2025 (Front Neurol, 16:1541276):
    10 RCTs, 757 patients (acupuncture vs sham only)
    → PSQI: MD -2.60 (95% CI -3.24 to -1.97, p<.00001)
    → ISI: MD -2.04 (95% CI -3.18 to -0.90, p=.0005)

    → Wake After Sleep Onset: MD -18.53 min

    28 RCTs, 2,063 women
    → vs sham (6 RCTs, 428 pts): PSQI MD -2.68 (95% CI -3.98 to -1.38)
    → Total Sleep Time vs sham: +57.17 min
    → GRADE: very low to moderate

  🟡 Consistent positive direction; quality low to moderate
```

#### Allergic Rhinitis

```
🟡 SUPERIOR TO SHAM; COMPARABLE TO ANTIHISTAMINES:

  He et al. 2022 (Eur J Med Res, 27:58):
    30 RCTs, 4,413 participants
    → vs sham (TNSS): MD -1.09 (95% CI -2.15 to -0.03)
    → vs sham (quality of life): SMD -0.95 (95% CI -1.17 to -0.73)

    → Significant antihistamine-sparing effect

  🟡 Positive but Trial Sequential Analysis not robust
```

```
      pooling across control types, but sham-controlled data weaker
    🔴 Evidence has evolved; sham-controlled data insufficient

  Hypertension:
    → 7 RCTs: SBP MD -6.73 mmHg, DBP MD -6.59 mmHg
    → Grade 1 HTN specifically: SBP -3.62, DBP -3.12 mmHg
    → Small evidence base, substantial heterogeneity
    → A 5-6 mmHg systolic reduction = 20-25% lower CVD risk if real
    🔴 Clinically meaningful if confirmed; insufficient evidence

  Obesity / weight management:
    → 64 RCTs overall; vs sham (8 RCTs, 403 pts):
      BMI MD -1.0 kg/m²; body weight MD -1.85 kg
    → Quality: generally poor
    🔴 Small effects; poor quality

  COPD:
    → GOLD 2021: acknowledged acupuncture as "palliative treatment
      in patients with advanced COPD" for dyspnea/quality of life
    → 9 SRs assessed: only 2 "low" quality, 7 "critically low"
    → No high-quality evidence confirms effectiveness
    🔴 Guideline acknowledgement, but evidence base weak

  Tinnitus:
    → Huang et al. 2021 (Acupunct Med, 39(4):323-333):
      8 RCTs, 504 participants
      Primary outcome (VAS): NO significant difference (p=0.06)
      Secondary outcomes (THI, TSI): significant improvement
    🔴 Inconclusive; primary outcome negative

  🔴 All conditions in this tier need larger, well-designed RCTs
     with appropriate sham controls before conclusions can be drawn
```

## §5 — PROPOSED MECHANISMS

### §5.1 — Traditional Explanation

```
⚠️ FRAMING: Traditional explanations are recorded AS DATA.
  See §1.3 for the complete traditional framework.

🔴 TRADITIONAL TCM MECHANISM:

  Acupuncture restores the smooth flow of Qi through the meridian
  network. Disease arises when Qi is blocked, stagnant, deficient,
  or rebellious. Needling at specific acupoints unblocks stagnation,
  tonifies deficiency, or drains excess, restoring Yin-Yang balance.

  The specific mechanism is determined by:
    → Point selection (§3): which pathways are accessed
    → Manipulation technique (§2.2): Bu (tonify) vs Xie (sedate)
    → De Qi achievement (§2.3): confirmation that Qi has arrived

  ASSESSMENT:
    → The empirical observations encoded in this framework
      (e.g., stimulating ST36 affects gastric motility) are
      measurable and confirmed by modern research (§4, §5.2)
    → The explanatory layer (Qi flow, meridian energy) does not
      correspond to known anatomy as described (→ A09)
    → TCM pattern differentiation may capture real physiological
      states (e.g., "Blood Stasis" ↔ hypercoagulability), but
      the mapping is incomplete and not systematically validated

  🔴 Traditional theory: recorded as data, not endorsed as mechanism
```

### §5.2 — Modern Research-Based Mechanisms

```
🟡 INTEGRATED MODEL: Acupuncture activates mechanisms at 6 levels
  simultaneously. Individual Track A files detail each system.
  This section integrates them into an ACUPUNCTURE-SPECIFIC model
  showing how needle stimulation engages all 6 levels in a
  coordinated cascade.

  The model explains:
    → Why acupuncture produces both LOCAL and SYSTEMIC effects
    → Why sham has partial effects (overlapping pathways)
    → Why different parameters produce different outcomes
    → Why effects persist beyond treatment duration
```

#### §5.2.1 — The 6-Level Integrated Model

```
🟡 FROM NEEDLE TO SYSTEMIC EFFECT — 6 LEVELS OF MECHANISM:

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  L1 — LOCAL TISSUE (seconds to minutes)                        │
  │                                                                │
  │  Needle insertion into tissue (80% at fascial planes, A03 §3.2)│
  │    → Collagen fiber winding around shaft (Langevin 2001)       │
  │    → Fibroblast cytoskeletal remodeling (Rho/Rac, A03 §2.3)   │
  │    → Mechanosensitive ion channels (Piezo1, TRPV4, A03 §2.3)  │
  │    → ATP release from fibroblasts → extracellular adenosine    │
  │      (Goldman 2010: 24-fold ↑, absent in A1-KO mice, A06 §1.2)│
  │    → Adenosine → A1 receptor → LOCAL ANALGESIA                │
  │                                                                │
  │  SIMULTANEOUSLY:                                               │
  │    → Controlled micro-tissue damage → DAMPs                   │
  │    → Mast cell degranulation (acupoints enriched, A05 §3.1)   │
  │      → Histamine, NGF, 5-HT, prostaglandins                   │
  │    → CGRP release → arteriolar vasodilation → ↑ blood flow    │
  │      (Lewis triple response, A04 §3.3)                        │
  │    → NO elevation at acupoints (Ma 2003/2017, A04 §3.4)       │
  │    → Local opioid release from immune cells + keratinocytes    │
  │      (β-endorphin, enkephalin, A06 §1.1)                      │
  │                                                                │
  │  NET EFFECT: local chemical milieu shifts from pro-nociceptive │
  │  to anti-nociceptive (A06 §4.1)                                │
  │                                                                │
  │  🟢 Individual mechanisms: well-documented                     │
  │  🟡 Integrated L1 model: synthesis across sources              │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  L2 — PERIPHERAL NERVE (milliseconds to seconds)               │
  │                                                                │
  │  Needle stimulation activates multiple fiber types:             │
  │    A-beta (Aβ): touch/pressure — mechanical De Qi component    │
  │      → Gate control input (→ L3)                               │
  │    A-delta (Aδ): fast pain — sharp quality if excessive        │
  │      → Descending modulation input (→ L4)                      │
  │    C fibers: slow/polymodal — aching/heavy De Qi component     │
  │      → Axon reflex feedback (→ L1 via CGRP/SP)                │
  │      → Descending modulation (→ L4)                            │
  │    PROKR2+ neurons (Liu 2021, Nature):                         │
  │      → Located in deep fascia (periosteum, muscle fascia)      │
  │      → ESSENTIAL for vagal-adrenal anti-inflammatory effect    │
  │      → Distribution: 16.7% lumbar DRG vs 6.1% thoracic DRG   │
  │      → Predicts which body regions produce which effects       │
  │      → (→ A02 §3.5 for full detail)                            │
  │                                                                │
  │  FIBER TYPE → DOWNSTREAM PATHWAY:                              │
  │    Aβ → spinal gate control (L3)                               │
  │    Aδ/C → descending modulation (L4)                           │
  │    PROKR2+ → vagal-adrenal axis (L4→L6)                       │
  │    = Different stimulation components activate DIFFERENT        │
  │      therapeutic pathways simultaneously                        │
  │                                                                │
  │  🟢 Fiber types: established (→ A01 §1.2)                      │
  │  🟢 PROKR2: Liu 2021 Nature — landmark discovery               │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  L3 — SPINAL CORD (milliseconds)                               │
```

#### §5.2.4 — Point Specificity: Molecular and Anatomical Basis

```
🟡 WHY DOES NEEDLE LOCATION MATTER?

  If effects were purely non-specific, location should not matter.
  Evidence for specificity comes from 4 independent lines:

  ① MOLECULAR: PROKR2 distribution (Liu 2021)
    → PROKR2 neurons concentrated in limb deep fascia
    → Sparse in trunk → predicts which sites drive vagal effects
    → Explains ST36 (limb) vs ST25 (abdomen) differences

  ② SEGMENTAL: Dermatome-viscerotome convergence (→ A07)
    → Back-Shu points match organ innervation (9/12, Dorsher)
    → Head zones = acupoint maximum points (Beissner 2011)
    → Kim 2017: neurogenic spots at cardiac acupoint locations
    → = Segmental anatomy explains organ-specific effects

  ③ STRUCTURAL: Anatomical convergence zones
    → 80% acupoints at fascial planes (Langevin 2002, A03)
    → Acupoints enriched in mast cells (A05)
    → Acupoints enriched in nerve endings (A01)
    → Points are NOT arbitrary — they occur at anatomically
      significant locations

  ④ EMPIRICAL: ATC and GERAC evidence (→ §9.2, §9.4)
    → ATC: verum > sham (0.15-0.23 SD) — specific effect exists
    → BUT: sham at acupoints ≈ verum for some conditions
    → GERAC: verum ≈ penetrating sham (both >> conventional)
    → = Specificity is REAL but PARTIAL and condition-dependent

  🟡 Synthesis: point specificity exists at molecular, segmental,
     and structural levels, but its magnitude varies by condition
     and stimulation type
```

#### §5.2.5 — Timing, Dose, and Neuroplasticity

```
🟡 DOSE-RESPONSE AND LONG-TERM EFFECTS:

  DOSE-RESPONSE (ATC data, MacPherson 2013, → §9.4):
    vs no-treatment: CLEAR dose-response
      More sessions: β=0.11, p=0.0007
      More needles: +0.33 SD per 5 additional needles
    vs sham: dose-response largely DISAPPEARS
      → The treatment PACKAGE is dose-dependent, but the SPECIFIC
        needle component shows little dose dependence
    Possible explanations: ceiling effect, sham dose-dependence,
    threshold model (adequate stimulation → no proportional gain)

  TREATMENT COURSE → NEUROPLASTICITY:
    Single session: acute (gate control, opioid) — hours
    Multiple sessions: cumulative neuroplastic effects:
      → BDNF/TrkB signaling ↑ (hippocampus, cortex — animal data)
      → Gray matter volume changes in pain-processing regions
      → DMN connectivity normalization
    Persistence: ~90% benefit maintained at 12 months vs no-
    treatment; ~70-85% vs sham (MacPherson 2017, → §9.4)
    → Argues for STRUCTURAL brain changes beyond acute effects

  🟢 Dose-response: ATC IPD meta-analysis
  🟡 Neuroplasticity: animal evidence strong, human limited
  🟢 Persistence: MacPherson 2017, 20 trials
```

### §7.3 — Key Philosophical Differences

```
🟡 FOUR COMPETING PARADIGMS USE THE SAME BASIC TOOL (needle):

  ① TCM PARADIGM: Treatment based on PATTERN, not disease.
    Same Western diagnosis → different acupoint prescriptions.
    Individualized by Zang-Fu assessment, pulse, tongue.
    Points selected from meridian system + Five Element logic.

  ② NEUROANATOMICAL PARADIGM (Western medical acupuncture):
    Treatment based on SEGMENTAL innervation + dermatomes.
    White 2009 (Acupunct Med, 27(1):33-35): "a therapeutic
    modality... using current knowledge of anatomy, physiology
    and pathology, and principles of evidence based medicine."
    No Qi, no meridians — nervous system modulation.
    235 acupoints concordant with Head zones (Beltran Molano 2014).

  ③ CONSTITUTIONAL PARADIGM (Sasang, Toyohari):
    Treatment based on INNATE BODY TYPE, not pattern or disease.
    Patient constitution determines which points/techniques are
    appropriate. Same disease in different constitutions →
    entirely different treatment.

  ④ MYOFASCIAL PARADIGM (dry needling):
    Treatment based on PALPABLE MUSCLE PATHOLOGY (taut bands,
    trigger points). No systemic theory. Local anatomical targets.
    Mechanism: motor endplate deactivation (→ A08).

  WHICH PARADIGM IS "RIGHT"?
    The 6-level model (§5.2.1) suggests EACH paradigm captures
    a DIFFERENT LEVEL of the mechanism:
    → Myofascial: L1-L2 (local tissue, peripheral nerve)
    → Neuroanatomical: L3 (segmental convergence)
    → TCM: L3-L4 (segmental + brainstem integration)
    → Constitutional: L5-L6 (cortical + systemic variation)
    → No single paradigm is complete; each emphasizes different
      aspects of the same multi-level phenomenon

  🟡 Paradigm comparison: our synthesis
  🔴 "Each paradigm captures a different level": hypothesis
```

## §8 — INDIVIDUAL VARIATION & CONTRAINDICATIONS

### §8.1 — Responder vs Non-Responder

```
🟡 NOT EVERYONE RESPONDS EQUALLY — PREDICTION IS AN ACTIVE FIELD:

  CLINICAL OBSERVATION:
    A consistent minority of patients do not respond to acupuncture.
    Identifying non-responders BEFORE treatment would save time,
    money, and enable better treatment matching.

  NEUROIMAGING PREDICTORS:

  Yang et al. 2020 (Front Neurol, 10.3389/fneur.2020.00111):
    n=41 migraine patients (19 responders, 22 non-responders)
    → Structural MRI (3T) + SVM classifier
    → 83% accuracy, 73% sensitivity, 85% specificity, AUC=0.79
    → Features: gray matter volume in 10 regions (frontal, temporal,
      parietal, cuneus, precuneus)
    → CAVEAT: n=41 is SMALL; no independent validation cohort

  Gao et al. 2025 (Ann Med):
    n=80 neck pain patients (48 responders, 32 non-responders)
    → resting-state fMRI + SVM → 85% accuracy
    → 117 functional connectivity edges predictive

  Yin et al. 2023 (Hum Brain Mapp, 10.1002/hbm.26449):
    n=89 + 58 independent validation (functional dyspepsia)
    → 88% classification accuracy, R²=0.61 in validation
    → Features: 37 resting-state FC features (orbitofrontal,
      caudate, hippocampus, anterior insula)

  Chan et al. 2026 (Front Neurol) — scoping review:
    36 articles (2011-2024), 17 on efficacy prediction
    → SVM dominant algorithm (11/17 studies)
    → Accuracy range across studies: 70-88%

  🟡 Neuroimaging prediction: consistent results but ALL studies
     have small samples and limited external validation
```

### §8.3 — Contraindications and Safety

```
🟢 SAFETY PROFILE SUMMARY (→ §9.5 for full data):

  ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS:
    → Uncontrolled bleeding disorders (hemophilia, severe
      thrombocytopenia, therapeutic anticoagulation at high INR)
    → Local infection, open wound, or burn at needle site
    → EA: cardiac pacemaker/defibrillator (electromagnetic
      interference risk)

  RELATIVE CONTRAINDICATIONS (use with caution):
    → Pregnancy: avoid empirically-contraindicated points
      (LI4, SP6, BL60, BL67 — historically linked to labor
      induction). Abdominal/sacral points in late pregnancy.
    → Severe needle phobia (consider laser or Shonishin)
    → Extreme frailty, cachexia, or immunosuppression
    → Metal allergies (rare with stainless steel; consider gold/silver)
    → Lymphedema (risk of infection at edematous sites)

  POPULATION-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS:
    → Children: reduce needle count, shallower depth, shorter
      retention. Shonishin (non-insertive) preferred <7 years.
    → Elderly: thinner needles, fewer points, gentler technique
    → First-time patients: risk of vasovagal response (needle
      syncope) — treat supine, not seated

  HIGH-RISK ANATOMICAL ZONES:
    → Thorax (BL, GB points): pneumothorax risk — most common
      serious adverse event. Oblique/shallow insertion mandatory.
    → Neck (anterior): carotid, jugular, trachea
    → Abdomen in thin patients: peritoneal penetration
    → Points near major vessels: epistaxis, hematoma

  SAFETY COMPARISON (→ §9.5 for detailed numbers):
    Acupuncture: 0.05 SAE per 10,000 treatments (White 2004)
    NSAIDs: ~16,500 GI deaths/year US (Singh 1998)
    Opioids: >47,000 overdose deaths/year US (CDC 2024)
    → Among the safest interventions in medicine

  🟢 Safety profile: large prospective surveys (>1M treatments)
  🟢 Contraindications: established clinical practice
```

#### §9.2.2 — Why Sham Acupuncture Is NOT Inert

```
🟢 THE CORE PROBLEM: SHAM CONTROLS HAVE PHYSIOLOGICAL ACTIVITY

  ① EFFECT SIZE DEPENDS ON SHAM TYPE:

  MacPherson, Vertosick, Vickers et al. 2014 (PLoS One, 9(4):e93739):
  Secondary analysis of ATC IPD data — effect sizes by sham type:

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Sham Type               │ N Trials │ SMD (95% CI)                 │
  │──────────────────────────┼──────────┼──────────────────────────────│
  │  Penetrating needle sham │ 9        │ 0.17 (0.11, 0.23) ⭐        │
  │  Non-penetrating needle  │ 4*       │ 0.43 (0.01, 0.85) ⭐        │
  │  Non-needle sham         │ 4        │ 0.38 (0.19, 0.57)           │
  └──────────────────────────┴──────────┴──────────────────────────────┘
  * After sensitivity analysis excluding outlier trials

  INTERPRETATION:
    → Penetrating sham yields SMALLER treatment effect (0.17) because the
      sham itself has substantial physiological activity — there is LESS
      ROOM for acupuncture to demonstrate superiority
    → Non-penetrating sham yields LARGER treatment effect (0.43) because
      it is more inert
    → Difference: -0.19 SD (p=0.058) after sensitivity analysis
    → Authors: "Penetrating needles appear to have important physiologic
      activity" and should be avoided as sham controls

  ② BIOMARKER EVIDENCE:

  Kim, Lee, Birch & Alraek 2022 (Front Neurosci, 16:834112):
    51 RCTs examining 36 serum biomarkers (inflammation, metabolic,
    neuromodulatory). Key finding: MOST biomarkers showed NO significant
    difference between sham and verum groups. Only 7/36 differed.
    → Sham produces SIMILAR physiological effects at the biomarker level

  ③ NEURAL MECHANISMS MAKING SHAM NON-INERT:

  Lundeberg et al. 2009 (Chin Med, 4:1):
    → Non-penetrating sham: light touch stimulates C-afferent mechano-
      receptors → insular cortex "limbic touch response"
    → Superficial needling: stimulates C-fibers → descending pain
      modulation pathways
    → ANY skin contact at ANY point activates cutaneous afferents

  🟢 MacPherson 2014: verified IPD analysis
  🟢 Kim 2022: systematic review of 51 RCTs
  🟢 Lundeberg 2009: physiological argument, well-supported
```

```
    Verum: 47.6%  |  Sham: 44.2%  |  Conventional: 27.4%
    Verum vs sham: p=0.39 (NOT significant)
    Verum vs conventional: p<0.001

  1,007 patients, 315 practices:
    Verum: 53.1%  |  Sham: 51.0%  |  Conventional: 29.1%
    Verum vs sham: RR 1.01 (NOT significant)

  GERAC Migraine (Diener et al. 2006, Lancet Neurol, 5(4):310-316):
  960 patients:
    Verum: 47% reduction  |  Sham: 39%  |  Drugs: 40%
    No significant differences between groups

  TWO COMPETING INTERPRETATIONS:

  ① SKEPTIC: Acupuncture = elaborate placebo. Needle placement irrelevant.
     Both groups benefit only from the ritual of treatment.

  ② ACTIVE-SHAM: The sham used in GERAC involved ACTUAL NEEDLE
     PENETRATION. Both groups received real needling. The 20-point
     superiority over conventional therapy CANNOT be explained by
     placebo alone. The trial failed to use an inert comparator.

  PRACTICAL OUTCOME: Germany began reimbursing acupuncture for LBP
```

### §9.3 — Publication Bias

```
🟡 SYSTEMATIC BIAS IN THE ACUPUNCTURE LITERATURE:

  ① GEOGRAPHIC PUBLICATION BIAS:

  Vickers 1998 (Controlled Clin Trials, 19(2):159-166):
    First documented geographic bias — acupuncture trials from China
    and several other Asian countries were UNIFORMLY positive.

  Wang et al. 2014 (J Altern Complement Med, 20(5):A129 — conference
    abstract, Society for Acupuncture Research):
    840 RCTs from 4 Chinese databases (CNKI, VIP, CBM, Wanfang):
    838 of 840 (99.8%) reported POSITIVE primary outcomes.
    → Methodological quality poor: only 5.9% reported allocation
      concealment, 4.9% reported blinding
    → Compared to ~50-60% positive rate in Western trials
    → Pattern seen across ALL Chinese medical research, not just
      acupuncture (Vickers 1998: China/Japan/HK/Taiwan = 100% positive)

  ② METHODOLOGICAL QUALITY:

  Wu et al. 2009 (Trials, 10:46, PMC2716312):
    Interviewed authors of 2,235 self-described "RCTs":
    → Only 207 (6.8%, 95% CI 5.9-7.7) were truly randomized
    → 1,913 authors (85.6%) did not fully understand randomization
    → Many used alternation, date of birth, or clinic registration
      order — NOT true randomization

  ③ HOW ATC ADDRESSED THIS BIAS:

  The Vickers ATC approach filters out poorly randomized studies:
    → REQUIRED verified adequate allocation concealment
    → Used individual patient data (IPD) — not summary data
    → Contacted original authors for methodological details
    → Excluded studies where concealment could not be verified

  RESULT: When restricted to high-quality Western trials with
  verified allocation concealment:
    → Acupuncture STILL > sham: 0.15-0.23 SD, p<.001
    → Acupuncture STILL > no-treatment: 0.42-0.57 SD, p<.001
    → Publication bias test (ATC 2012): P=.02 for sham comparisons
      (suggestive); after Vas exclusion P=.07; after excluding small
      studies P=.30

  ④ GEOGRAPHIC SCALE:
    China: ~28% of global acupuncture trials, near-100% positive rate
    → Any meta-analysis that includes Chinese trials without quality
      filters will be BIASED toward positive results
    → ATC's allocation concealment requirement effectively excludes
      most low-quality Chinese trials

  🟡 Publication bias: documented pattern, not unique to acupuncture
  🟢 ATC methodology: explicitly designed to address this
```

### §9.4 — Placebo vs Specific Effects

```
🟡 THE CENTRAL QUESTION: IS ACUPUNCTURE "MORE THAN PLACEBO"?

  ① THE SKEPTICAL POSITION:

  Colquhoun & Novella 2013 (Anesth Analg, 116(6):1360-1363):
    Title: "Acupuncture Is Theatrical Placebo"
    Published as "Con editorial" in pro/con debate format.
    Argument: meta-analytic data shows acupuncture lacks compelling
    evidence of superiority over sham; effects are due to the
    elaborate ritual of treatment.

  ② COUNTER-EVIDENCE — ANIMAL MODELS:

  Goldman et al. 2010 (Nat Neurosci, 13(7):883-888):
    → Acupuncture at Zusanli (mice): 24-fold increase in local
      adenosine during 30-minute treatment
    → A1-receptor knockout mice: acupuncture FAILED to suppress pain
    → Wild-type mice: pain reduced by two-thirds
    → Deoxycoformycin (adenosine degradation inhibitor): adenosine
      accumulation nearly tripled, treatment duration extended 3×
    → SIGNIFICANCE: animal models eliminate placebo as explanation
    → Cross-reference: → A06 §1.2 (adenosine pathway)

  ③ COMPONENTS OF PLACEBO:

  Kaptchuk et al. 2008 (BMJ, 336(7651):999-1003):
    Three-arm RCT in IBS, N=262, single-blind:
      Arm 1 — Waitlist (no treatment):           28% adequate relief
      Arm 2 — Sham acupuncture, limited interaction: 44%
      Arm 3 — Sham acupuncture, augmented interaction: 62%
    → Each step adds ~16% improvement
    → The therapeutic RELATIONSHIP contributes independently of
      the needle
    → NOTE: ALL sham — no real acupuncture arm in this study

  ④ EXPECTATIONS AND PAIN:

  Vase et al. 2013 (Pain, 154(9):1659-1667):
    N=101, dental pain (third molar extraction):
    → Expected pain levels accounted for up to 69.8% of pain
      intensity variance in the active acupuncture group (late phase)
    → No significant difference in pain between active and placebo
      acupuncture (P=0.240)
    → BUT both > no treatment

  ⑤ OBJECTIVE vs SUBJECTIVE OUTCOMES:

  Wechsler et al. 2011 (NEJM, 365(2):119-126):
    Four-arm crossover in asthma (N=46):
      Albuterol:          FEV1 +20%  |  Subjective: 50% improvement
      Placebo inhaler:    FEV1 +7%   |  Subjective: 45%
      Sham acupuncture:   FEV1 +7%   |  Subjective: 46%
      No intervention:    FEV1 +7%   |  Subjective: 21%
    → Only albuterol improved objective lung function
    → Sham acupuncture = placebo = same subjective relief
    → IMPLICATION: acupuncture's effects on subjective outcomes
      (pain, nausea) may include a significant non-specific component
    → CAVEAT: study used SHAM acupuncture, not real — cannot
      distinguish between ritual effects and needle-specific effects

  ⑥ PERSISTENCE ARGUES AGAINST PURE PLACEBO:

  MacPherson, Vertosick, Vickers et al. 2017 (Pain, 158(5):784-793):
    20 trials, 6,376 patients with chronic pain follow-up data:
    → vs no-acupuncture control: diminution of 0.011 SD per 3 months
      (p=0.4, NOT significant) → ~90% of benefit maintained at 12 months
    → vs sham: diminution of 0.025 SD per 3 months (p=0.05)
      → ~50% diminution at 12 months overall
      → BUT driven by neck pain outlier; excluding neck: only ~28-30%
        diminution at 12 months
    → Placebo effects typically DECAY faster than this
    → 2018 update: approximately 15% diminution at 1 year

  ⑦ DOSE-RESPONSE PARADOX:

  MacPherson et al. 2013 (PLoS One, 8(10):e77438):
    ATC dataset (29 trials, 17,922 patients):
    → vs no-acupuncture: CLEAR dose-response
      More sessions: beta=0.11 (p=0.0007)
      More needles: +0.33 SD per 5 additional needles (p=0.010)
    → vs sham: dose-response largely DISAPPEARS
      No significant effect modification for sessions or needles
    → IMPLICATION: dose matters for the OVERALL therapeutic package
      (ritual + context + needling), but the SPECIFIC needle
      component shows little dose dependence

  SYNTHESIS — WHERE THE EVIDENCE LANDS:

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  WHAT WE CAN SAY:                                                  │
  │  ✓ Acupuncture > no treatment: 0.42-0.57 SD (p<.001, ATC)         │
  │  ✓ Acupuncture > sham: 0.15-0.23 SD (p<.001, ATC)                 │
  │  ✓ Animal models confirm non-placebo mechanism (Goldman 2010)      │
  │  ✓ Effects persist at 12 months (MacPherson 2017)                  │
  │  ✓ Sham is NOT inert — systematically underestimates effect        │
  │                                                                    │
  │  WHAT REMAINS UNCLEAR:                                             │
  │  ? How much is needle-specific vs ritual/context/expectation?      │
  │  ? Dose-response disappears vs sham — what does this mean?         │
  │  ? Subjective outcomes may overstate specific effects (Wechsler)   │
  │                                                                    │
  │  THE ATC CONCLUSION:                                               │
  │  "Acupuncture is more than a placebo" — the specific effect is     │
  │  SMALL but STATISTICALLY SIGNIFICANT and PERSISTENT, on top of     │
  │  a LARGE non-specific effect from the therapeutic ritual.          │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  🟢 Individual studies: verified citations and numbers
  🟡 Synthesis: integrating evidence across multiple methodologies
```

## §10 — OPEN QUESTIONS

```
🟡 WHAT REMAINS UNEXPLAINED OR UNRESOLVED:

  Q1 — CAN THE SHAM DEBATE BE RESOLVED?
    Sham laser (deactivated device) is the most inert option,
    but it eliminates the MECHANICAL component entirely.
    No perfect sham exists for a therapy that involves skin
    penetration. The Luo 2025 quantification (18.9% of effect
    from penetrating sham) is a step forward, but no consensus
    exists on how to incorporate this into trial design.
    → §9.2 for full sham debate

  Q2 — WHAT IS THE OPTIMAL DOSE-RESPONSE FOR EACH CONDITION?
    No standardized dose guidelines exist. ATC shows dose-response
    vs no-treatment but NOT vs sham. How many sessions, how many
    needles, what frequency, for which condition — all remain
    empirically determined without rigorous dose-finding studies.

  Q3 — CAN RESPONDERS BE PREDICTED PRE-TREATMENT?
    Neuroimaging (70-88% accuracy, §8.1) and genetic (COMT,
    GABRB3, §8.2) predictors show promise but ALL have small
    samples and no external validation. Translating these to
    clinical practice requires prospective validation trials.

  Q4 — DO DIFFERENT TRADITIONS WORK THROUGH DIFFERENT MECHANISMS?
    Japanese shallow/non-insertive vs Chinese deep/strong De Qi:
    do they activate different levels of the 6-level model?
    Toyohari (contact only) presumably activates only L1-L2;
    deep TCM needling activates L1-L4. If both produce clinical
    effects, this constrains which levels are NECESSARY.
    No direct mechanistic comparison studies exist.

  Q5 — WHAT ARE THE LONG-TERM NEUROPLASTIC EFFECTS?
    MacPherson 2017 shows ~90% benefit maintained at 12 months.
    BDNF/TrkB upregulation demonstrated in animal models.
    Gray matter volume changes documented in small human studies.
    Missing: large longitudinal studies tracking structural brain
    changes over years of regular acupuncture treatment.

  Q6 — CAN PRECISION ACUPUNCTURE OUTPERFORM TRADITIONAL
       PATTERN DIFFERENTIATION?
    AI-guided point selection (Nie et al. 2026) can match
    experienced practitioners for some conditions. Can ML-based
    optimization of parameters (point selection, frequency,
    intensity, session count) systematically outperform TCM
    clinical reasoning? If yes, this would suggest that TCM
    patterns encode measurable physiological states that can
    be captured computationally.

  Q7 — WILL TARA RESOLVE ACUPOINT IDENTITY?
    The TARA project (Napadow/Harris/Helmer) aims to build a
    topological atlas of acupoints using spatial omics, advanced
    imaging, and standardized ontology. If successful, this could
    definitively answer whether acupoints have a unique molecular
    identity distinct from surrounding tissue. However, NCCIH
    faces existential political threat (FY2026-2027 elimination
    proposals), putting all NIH-funded acupuncture research at risk.

  Q8 — WILL PHARMACOPUNCTURE PROVE SUPERIOR TO PLAIN NEEDLING?
    Bee venom (melittin, apamin) adds pharmacological action to
    mechanical needling. 2025 SR (Sung): 20 RCTs suggest benefit
    for musculoskeletal pain but quality is LOW. Can combining
    bioactive injection with acupoint stimulation create a
    synergistic effect beyond either alone? If so, this would
    support the multi-system activation model (§5.3) — adding
    another level of intervention.

  🟡 All questions: genuine unknowns in the field
```
